# 46 | Co-Investments: Investing Alongside the Fund

*For forty-five articles, the LP has always bought the fund — a diversified pool, a blind commitment, a ten-year wait. This one buys the deal. A co-investment is a direct stake in a single portfolio company, made alongside the fund, at the same price, usually with no management fee and no carried interest. It is the cheapest capital in private equity and, by some measures, the most dangerous. The fee saving is arithmetic. The catch is everything the fund was doing for you that you can no longer see.*

> **Learning goals.** After this article you can: (1) explain what a co-investment is, how it differs from a fund commitment, and why it is usually fee-free; (2) compute exactly what the fee and carry saving is worth on a single deal, and what it costs in concentration; (3) explain why a GP offers co-investments and why an LP wants them; (4) describe the adverse selection question — does the GP offer you its best deals or its worst? — and summarise what the two main academic studies actually found; (5) judge a co-investment offer with a short checklist instead of a gut feeling.
>
> **Prerequisites.** Article 06 (DCF I) for discounting and IRR; article 08 (LBO) for the fund structure, management fees, carried interest, and the hurdle rate; article 41 (secondary buyouts) for the fund-life clock and concentration limits; article 45 (GP stakes) for how a manager's fee income works and why fundraising is the engine.

## 1. The Idea in One Paragraph

The normal private equity deal is a package: you commit capital to a fund, the manager picks fifteen or twenty companies, and you pay 2 percent a year plus 20 percent of the profits for the service. A **co-investment** unwraps the package. When the fund buys a company, the manager offers its largest limited partners the chance to invest directly in that same company, on the same terms, alongside the fund — an extra cheque that sits next to the fund's cheque, in the same equity, at the same entry price. The defining feature is the price of the service: typically **no management fee and no carried interest**. You are buying the deal, not the manager. That sounds like free money, and on the fee line it is. The catch is that you are also buying one company instead of fifteen, deciding in days instead of months, and doing your own diligence on a deal someone else found. The fund was selling you diversification, selection, and time. The co-investment strips all three away and hands you the saving.

## 2. Anatomy: What a Co-Investment Actually Is

A co-investment starts with a size problem. A fund has concentration limits — often no single deal can be more than 10 to 15 percent of the fund — and it has a finite cheque size. When a good deal is bigger than the fund can hold alone, the manager has three options: syndicate it to other funds, walk away, or offer a slice to its own LPs. The co-investment is the third option. The LP signs a separate agreement for that one deal, wires the money when the deal closes, and owns its slice directly. The fund manages the company; the co-investor is a passenger, with information rights and usually no vote.

There are three shapes it takes in practice.

**Deal-by-deal co-investment** is the classic form: a one-off offer, tied to a single transaction, extended to a small group of large LPs — usually the ones who committed the most to the fund, or who asked for co-investment rights when they signed up. Minimum tickets are typically $5 to $25 million, and the decision window is short, often a week or two. You are underwriting a live deal on someone else's timetable.

**Co-investment funds** are a second route: a fund whose only strategy is to co-invest alongside other funds' deals. The irony is deliberate — you pay a fee to a co-investment fund to avoid paying a fee to a buyout fund. The fee is usually lower, often around 1 percent and 10 percent carry, and the pitch is that the co-investment fund does the diligence and builds the portfolio of ten or fifteen deals that a single LP cannot build alone.

| | Fund commitment | Co-investment |
|---|---|---|
| What you own | A share of 15–20 companies | A share of one company |
| Management fee | ~2% a year on committed capital | Usually zero |
| Carried interest | ~20% of profits above an ~8% hurdle | Usually zero |
| Diversification | Built in | None — you supply it yourself, deal by deal |
| Decision speed | Months of fundraising, then blind | Days, on a live deal |
| Control | None | Information rights, usually no vote |
| Diligence | The GP's job | Still the GP's job — but you should check it |

One mechanical point matters before we do the numbers. "No fee, no carry" is the norm, but it is a negotiated term, not a law. Some managers charge a small fee on co-investments; some offer co-investment rights only to LPs who commit above a threshold to the main fund. And the saving is not free in the deeper sense: the LP still pays for its own investment team, its own lawyers, and its own diligence. The cost of doing the work moves from the manager's invoice to the LP's payroll.

## 3. The Worked Example: What the Fee Saving Is Worth

Take a single deal and run it two ways. You have $10 million to put to work. The fund buys a company, the equity doubles over five years — a 2.0x multiple — and you either invest the $10 million directly alongside the fund, or you put the same $10 million through the fund and let it invest in the same deal.

**Route A: co-invest directly.** No fee, no carry. Your $10 million becomes $20 million.

```math
\text{Co-invest net} = \$10\text{ million} \times 2.0 = \$20.0\text{ million}
```

```math
\text{Co-invest IRR} = 2.0^{1/5} - 1 \approx 14.9\%
```

**Route B: through the fund.** The same $10 million goes into the same deal at the same entry and exit. Gross, it also becomes $20 million. Then the fund's economics take their cut. The management fee runs at 2 percent of committed capital a year — over five years, that is $1.0 million. The carried interest is 20 percent of the profit above an 8 percent annual hurdle. The hurdle value of your $10 million after five years is:

```math
\text{Hurdle value} = \$10\text{ million} \times 1.08^5 = \$10\text{ million} \times 1.469 = \$14.69\text{ million}
```

```math
\text{Profit above hurdle} = \$20.00\text{ million} - \$14.69\text{ million} = \$5.31\text{ million}
```

```math
\text{Carry} = 20\% \times \$5.31\text{ million} = \$1.06\text{ million}
```

```math
\text{Fund-route net} = \$20.00 - \$1.00 - \$1.06 = \$17.94\text{ million}
```

```math
\text{Fund-route multiple} = \frac{\$17.94}{\$10.00} = 1.79\text{x}
```

```math
\text{Fund-route IRR} = 1.79^{1/5} - 1 \approx 12.4\%
```

Same deal, same entry, same exit. The co-investor keeps 2.00x and a 14.9 percent IRR; the fund investor keeps 1.79x and a 12.4 percent IRR. The fee drag is about **250 basis points a year**, every year, on every deal. That is the whole pitch for co-investing in one table, and it is why co-investment rights are one of the most negotiated terms in a fund's limited partnership agreement — and why the largest LPs, the sovereign wealth funds and the big pensions, fight hardest for them.

Two honest caveats before you take the 250 basis points to the bank. First, the arithmetic above is a deal-level approximation: in reality the management fee is charged on committed capital across the whole fund, and carry is calculated at the fund level on the entire portfolio, usually with a European waterfall — you do not pay carry deal by deal. The direction and rough size of the drag are right; the exact cents depend on the fund's other deals. Second, the comparison assumes the same gross return in both routes. That is exactly the assumption the rest of this article interrogates: is the deal you are offered the same quality as the deal the fund keeps for itself?

## 4. The Price of Concentration

The fee saving is the visible half of the trade. The invisible half is what the fund was quietly doing for you: spreading your money across many deals so that one failure does not sink you. Here is how much that insurance is worth.

Suppose each deal, independently, has a 70 percent chance of doubling your money (2.0x) and a 30 percent chance of halving it (0.5x). The expected multiple is the same whether you hold one deal or fifteen:

```math
\text{Expected multiple} = 0.70 \times 2.0 + 0.30 \times 0.5 = 1.55\text{x}
```

But the risk is not the same at all. With a single co-investment, your chance of losing money is the full 30 percent — one bad outcome and you are down. With a fund of fifteen equal deals, you lose money only if fewer than five of the fifteen deals succeed:

```math
\text{Portfolio multiple} < 1.0 \iff 2g + 0.5(15 - g) < 15 \iff g < 5
```

where $g$ is the number of successful deals. With each deal succeeding independently with probability 0.70, the chance of five or fewer successes out of fifteen is about **0.07 percent** — less than one-tenth of one percent. Same expected return, 1.55x either way. The single deal loses money 30 times out of 100. The fifteen-deal portfolio loses money roughly 7 times out of 10,000.

That is the real price of the co-investment, stated precisely: you are trading a 250-basis-point fee saving for a jump in your chance of losing money from under 0.1 percent to 30 percent on that slice of capital. The trade is not obviously wrong — 250 basis points a year is a lot of money, and a large LP can rebuild the diversification itself by doing ten or fifteen co-investments. But it is a trade, and the investors who get hurt are the ones who treat the fee saving as free and forget that the diversification has to be rebuilt, deal by deal, out of their own pocket and their own team. A co-investment programme of one deal is not a strategy. It is a bet.

## 5. Why Would a GP Offer You Its Best Deal?

This is the question that decides whether the fee saving survives contact with reality. Think about it from the manager's side. A GP earns fees on the capital in its fund and carry on the fund's profits. Every dollar of a deal it hands to a co-investor is a dollar that earns the GP no fee and no carry. So a GP that offers you a slice of a deal is giving up money to do it. Why would it?

**Because it has to.** The most common reason is size. The deal is bigger than the fund's concentration limit allows, or bigger than the fund's remaining dry powder covers. The GP needs a bigger cheque than it can write, and the choice is between sharing the deal and losing it. These are often the largest, most competitive deals — not the worst ones.

**Because it wants the relationship.** Co-investment access is currency. A GP that wants a sovereign wealth fund in its next fund, or wants an existing LP to increase its commitment, offers co-investment rights as part of the bargain. The co-investment is not a standalone transaction; it is a piece of a multi-decade relationship, and the price of the relationship is measured in future fund commitments.

**Because it wants speed and certainty.** A co-investor who can decide in a week and wire the money on close is worth more than a syndicate partner who needs a month of committee meetings. In a competitive auction, the ability to show the seller a fully funded bid — fund plus committed co-investors — can be the difference between winning and losing.

All three reasons are benign. But notice what they have in common: in every case, the GP is choosing which deals to offer. The deals it can hold entirely inside the fund — the ones that fit the concentration limit, that it does not need help funding — it keeps. The deals it offers are, by construction, the ones it cannot or will not hold alone. That selection is the heart of the co-investment question, and it is an empirical question, not a theoretical one. It cannot be settled by logic. It has to be settled by data.

## 6. What the Research Actually Finds

Two studies have tried to settle it, using very different data, and they disagree — which is exactly what makes the question interesting.

**Fang, Ivashina, and Lerner (2015)**, "The Disintermediation of Financial Markets: Direct Investing in Private Equity," *Journal of Financial Economics* 116(1), 160–178, studied twenty years of direct investing by seven large institutional investors, covering 390 direct investments made between 1991 and 2011. Their headline result is stark: co-investments **underperform** the corresponding funds they co-invest with. On 103 co-investments, the mean IRR was **9.0 percentage points lower** than the corresponding fund's IRR, and the money multiple was **0.22x lower**. Their explanation is adverse selection: GPs offered co-investments disproportionately at market peaks, when expected returns were lowest, and kept the better deals inside the fund. Strikingly, the same study found the opposite for *solo* direct investments — deals the institutions originated themselves, without a fund — which outperformed fund benchmarks. The problem, on this reading, is not direct investing. It is being handed someone else's deal flow.

**Braun, Jenkinson, and Schemmerl (2020)**, "Adverse Selection and the Performance of Private Equity Co-Investments," *Journal of Financial Economics* 136(1), 44–62, came back with a much bigger dataset: 1,016 co-investments made by 458 investors, drawn from 464 funds that made 13,430 investments in total, built by matching Capital IQ's transactions database against fund data. Their headline result is the opposite: gross return distributions of co-investments and other fund deals are **similar** — no evidence of adverse selection. Because co-investments carry lower costs, they then simulated net returns and found that **reasonably sized portfolios of co-investments significantly out-performed fund returns**. A 2015 survey they cite found that half of private equity investors already made co-investments, and three-quarters of those intended to increase or maintain the activity.

How do you hold two opposite findings in your head at once? Start with the differences. Fang, Ivashina, and Lerner had 103 co-investments from seven institutions that were willing to share their full cash-flow records — a small, possibly unrepresentative sample, and one drawn from an era (the 1990s and 2000s) when co-investing was newer and less competitive. Braun, Jenkinson, and Schemmerl had ten times as many deals, from a commercial database rather than volunteers, covering a later and broader market. Sample size and construction alone can explain a lot of the gap. There is also a timing story: Fang et al. found the performance of direct investments deteriorated sharply in the 2000s as the market got more competitive — the information advantage that made solo deals work in the 1990s had been competed away. The co-investment market of 2020 is not the co-investment market of 2005.

The honest synthesis is this: the fee saving is real and large — both studies agree on that, and the worked example in section 3 shows you why. Whether the deals you are offered are average, worse than average, or better is genuinely uncertain, and the best available evidence has swung from "worse" to "about the same" as the datasets got bigger. The one finding both studies support is the portfolio point: Braun et al. show the outperformance only appears for *reasonably sized portfolios* of co-investments, and Fang et al.'s worst results are concentrated in one-off deals offered at market peaks. The fee advantage compounds across a portfolio. The adverse selection risk concentrates in a single deal.

## 7. How to Judge a Co-Investment Offer

When the call comes — a deal, a week to decide, a data room the size of a small country — here is the short checklist that replaces the gut feeling.

**1. Would you do this deal on its own merits?** Underwrite it as if the GP were not involved: the business, the entry price, the leverage, the exit. If the only reason to say yes is that it is fee-free, say no. A fee-free bad deal is still a bad deal.

**2. Is the GP's money in at the same price?** Check that the fund is investing alongside you, in the same security, at the same entry price. If the GP is offering you a slice it is not taking itself, ask why — that is the adverse selection alarm, and it should be loud.

**3. Why is this deal being offered?** Size, speed, and relationship are good answers. "We want to share the risk" is an answer that deserves a follow-up question. The benign reasons leave a paper trail — a concentration limit, an auction timetable. Ask to see it.

**4. Size it like a portfolio, not a deal.** Cap any single co-investment at a small share of your private equity allocation, and judge the programme on ten or fifteen deals, not one. If you cannot build to that number, a co-investment fund — paying a small fee to get the diversification back — may be the better route.

**5. Read the terms, not just the price.** Confirm no fee and no carry in writing. Check whether the co-investment carries any side obligations — some managers expect co-investors to support the next fund, or to take a pro-rata share of follow-on capital. And confirm your exit: you sell when the fund sells, on the fund's timetable, not yours.

**6. Keep score.** Track your co-investment portfolio's net returns against your fund portfolio's net returns, deal by deal, year by year. The Fang versus Braun debate is about other people's data. The only number that settles it for you is your own.

## 8. Common Misconceptions

**"Co-investment is free money."** It is fee-free, not risk-free. You pay in concentration, in diligence, and in decision risk — a week to underwrite a deal the GP spent months on. The fee saving is real, but it is a trade, not a gift.

**"The GP offers its best deals to its best LPs."** The GP offers the deals it cannot hold alone, and it offers them to the LPs whose relationship it values most. Those two filters overlap but they are not the same filter. The best deal in the fund — the one that fits neatly inside the concentration limit — usually never gets offered at all.

**"No carry means the GP doesn't care about my money."** The GP earns carry on the fund's share of the same deal, so its incentive on the deal itself is intact. What it does not have is a direct financial incentive on *your* slice — which is why the governance terms and the GP's own commitment matter more, not less.

**"One great co-investment proves the strategy works."** One deal proves nothing except that one deal worked. The fee advantage only shows up across a portfolio, and the concentration risk only shows up in the deal that fails. Judge the programme after ten deals, not one.

**"Co-investing can replace fund investing."** It cannot, for a structural reason: you only get co-investment offers because you are an LP in the fund. Cut the fund commitment and the deal flow stops with it. Co-investment is a complement to the fund relationship — a way to deploy more capital alongside a manager you already trust — not a substitute for it.

**"The research has settled whether co-investments outperform."** It has not. Two careful studies, both in the *Journal of Financial Economics*, reach opposite conclusions on adverse selection. The honest position is the one in section 6: the fee saving is certain, the deal quality is uncertain, and the portfolio is what makes the difference.

## 9. Test Yourself

**1.** You have $5 million. A deal returns 1.8x over four years. Compare (a) investing it as a co-investment, no fee, no carry, with (b) investing it through a fund charging 2% a year on committed capital plus 20% carry above an 8% hurdle. What is the net multiple and IRR in each route, and how big is the fee drag?

*Answer: (a) Co-investment: $5m × 1.8 = $9.0m, a 1.80x multiple. IRR = 1.8^(1/4) − 1 ≈ 15.8%. (b) Fund route: gross is also $9.0m. Fees = 2% × $5m × 4 years = $0.40m. Hurdle value = $5m × 1.08^4 = $5m × 1.360 = $6.80m. Profit above the hurdle = $9.00m − $6.80m = $2.20m, so carry = 20% × $2.20m = $0.44m. Net = $9.00m − $0.40m − $0.44m = $8.16m, a 1.63x multiple. IRR = 1.63^(1/4) − 1 ≈ 13.0%. The fee drag is about 280 basis points a year — and notice it is larger here than in the 2.0x example, because the lower gross return leaves the hurdle taking a bigger bite.*

**2.** Each deal independently has a 25% chance of losing everything (0.0x) and a 75% chance of returning 1.8x. What is the chance a single-deal co-investment loses everything? What is the chance a fund holding ten such deals loses everything? What is the expected multiple in each case?

*Answer: Single deal: 25% — one failure and you are wiped out. Ten-deal fund: all ten must fail, which happens with probability 0.25^10 ≈ 0.0001% — about one in a million. Expected multiple is the same either way: 0.75 × 1.8 + 0.25 × 0 = 1.35x. Diversification does not change the expected return; it changes the shape of the distribution, and the shape is what keeps you in the game.*

**3.** Fang, Ivashina, and Lerner (2015) found that co-investments underperformed their corresponding funds by a mean IRR of 9.0 percentage points and a money multiple of 0.22x. Explain the adverse selection mechanism they proposed, and why it would produce exactly this pattern.

*Answer: The GP chooses which deals to offer for co-investment. If it keeps the best deals inside the fund — where it earns fees and carry on the full amount — and offers co-investments mainly when it needs outside capital, which happens disproportionately at market peaks and on the largest, hardest-to-fund deals, then the offered set is worse than the fund's average deal. The co-investor pays no fees, but it pays by getting a worse gross deal: the fee saving (worth roughly 2–3 percentage points a year) is more than wiped out by the selection gap (9.0 percentage points in their sample). The mechanism is selection, not fees — which is why their solo investments, where the LP chose the deal itself, outperformed.*

**4.** Braun, Jenkinson, and Schemmerl (2020) reach the opposite conclusion on adverse selection, on a sample of 1,016 co-investments rather than 103. Suppose you are an LP deciding whether to build a co-investment programme. How should this disagreement change your decision — and what would you track to find out who is right for you?

*Answer: It should not change the fee arithmetic — the saving is real in both studies — but it should change how much you trust the deal flow. Treat the deal quality as uncertain, not as a known discount. The practical response is the portfolio: build to ten or more co-investments so that no single selection mistake dominates, cap each one small, and insist on the checklist in section 7 (same price as the fund, GP money alongside, a benign reason for the offer). Then track your own numbers: your co-investment portfolio's net IRR and multiple versus your fund portfolio's, deal by deal. Two studies disagreeing about other investors is interesting; your own ten-deal track record is decisive.*
