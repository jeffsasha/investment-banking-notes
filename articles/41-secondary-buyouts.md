# 41 | Secondary Buyouts: When One Sponsor Sells to Another

*In article 08, a leveraged buyout ended with an exit: the sponsor fixes the company, then sells it — to a strategic buyer, or to the public market through an IPO. That is the textbook version. But look at what actually happens most often these days, and the buyer is neither a strategist nor the stock market. It is another private equity firm. One sponsor sells the company to a second sponsor, which buys it with a fresh pile of debt, runs its own playbook for five years, and then — very often — sells it to a third sponsor. The company never goes public, never gets absorbed into a bigger corporation. It just changes private equity owners, like a house being flipped from one landlord to the next. This article is about that transaction: the secondary buyout. Why the first sponsor sells, why the second one buys, why the numbers get harder the second time around, and what the research says about whether any of this actually makes money.*

> **Learning goals.** After this article you can: (1) define a secondary buyout (SBO) and explain how it differs from a primary buyout, an IPO exit, and a strategic sale; (2) explain the seller's and the buyer's reasons for doing the deal, including the fund-life clock that forces many of them; (3) walk through the mechanics — auctions, diligence advantages, and staple financing — that make SBOs faster and more certain than other exits; (4) compute why the second buyer needs faster growth than the first to earn the same return, and show the arithmetic; (5) describe what the research finds about when SBOs create value and when they destroy it; (6) judge a proposed SBO from the point of view of the buyer, the seller, and the LP.
>
> **Prerequisites.** Article 08 (LBO) for sources and uses, MOIC, IRR, and leverage; article 11 (sell-side M&A) for how an auction runs; article 25 (leveraged finance) for how the debt is raised; article 39 (take-privates) for the primary buyout as the starting point; article 40 (dividend recapitalizations) for the seller's main alternative to selling at all.

## 1. The Idea in One Paragraph

A **secondary buyout** — an **SBO**, sometimes called a **sponsor-to-sponsor deal** — is a leveraged buyout in which the seller is itself a private equity fund. The buyer is another private equity fund. Nothing about the company's operations changes hands to a strategic owner, and no shares are sold to the public. Ownership passes from one set of financial investors to another, the company takes on a new — usually larger — pile of debt, and the clock restarts: new fund, new five-year hold, new exit plan.

That is the whole transaction, and it is worth pausing on how odd it is. The classic story of private equity is that a sponsor buys a company, improves it, and sells it to someone who values it more — a strategist who can extract synergies, or public investors who will pay a higher multiple. In an SBO, the buyer is a firm with the same business model as the seller, the same tools, the same cost of capital, and often the same playbook. If the first sponsor already did the obvious improvements, what is left for the second one to do? And if the company is such a good investment, why is the first sponsor selling it at all? Those two questions are the whole puzzle of the secondary buyout, and the rest of this article is an attempt to answer them honestly — including the answer the industry does not like, which is that sometimes the deal is not about the company at all.

The scale is what makes the question unavoidable. SBOs were a rarity in the 1990s. By the late 2000s they had grown to roughly 40% of private equity exits (Strömberg, 2008, as cited in Degeorge, Martin & Phalippou, 2016), and they have stayed at roughly that level since. A transaction type that accounts for two in five exits cannot be dismissed as a curiosity. Either it works, or the industry has a very large, very persistent problem.

## 2. Two Sides of the Same Deal

An SBO only happens if both sides want it. Their reasons are different, and — this matters — they are not equally respectable.

### Why the seller sells

**The fund-life clock.** This is the big one, and the least discussed in polite company. A private equity fund has a fixed life, typically ten years, with an investment period of about five. When a fund is in year seven or eight, it cannot simply hold its companies forever: the fund agreement requires it to return capital to its limited partners (LPs) and wind down. A portfolio company that still has value in it must be sold — not because the work is finished, but because the calendar says so. The seller is, in the language of the research, *under pressure to exit*. This is the single most important structural fact about SBOs, and section 5 shows it drives much of what goes wrong.

**The playbook is done.** A sponsor's edge is usually a specific set of skills: cost-cutting, pricing, add-on acquisitions, professionalising a family business. Once those moves are made, the marginal value the sponsor can still add falls toward zero. The rational move is to sell to someone whose skills are different — a fund that specialises in international expansion, say, or in digital transformation. This is the optimistic story, and it is real: funds do specialise, and the handoff can be genuinely productive. But note the asymmetry: the seller's claim that "our work here is done" is easy to make and hard to verify from the outside.

**Fundraising needs a realisation.** Like the dividend recap in article 40, an exit converts an unrealised gain into a distributed one, and the DPI (distributions to paid-in capital) that results is the number LPs actually look at. The seller's incentive here is about the *fund's* franchise, not the *company's* future.

**The other exits are closed.** An IPO needs a receptive equity market; a strategic sale needs a strategic buyer with appetite and a balance sheet. When the IPO window is shut and no strategist is bidding, the SBO is the exit that remains. The research confirms this pattern: SBOs are more likely precisely when the equity market is "cold" and the debt market is favourable (Wang, 2012). That is a useful clue. If the best buyers are unavailable, selling to another financial buyer is the fallback — and the fallback price is set by the debt market, not by the company's fundamentals.

### Why the buyer buys

**A known asset with lower search costs.** A company owned by another PE firm is, in a practical sense, already for sale. Its financials are audited, its management is professional, its reporting is monthly and clean. The buyer does not have to hunt for the opportunity, persuade a founder to sell, or clean up the books. The search costs are lower, and so are the adverse-selection problems: a PE-owned company is a company that has already survived one round of professional diligence. This is a genuine advantage, and it is why SBOs can be rational even at full prices.

**Complementary skills.** This is the mirror image of the seller's "playbook is done" argument, and it is the best case for the SBO. The first sponsor cut costs and fixed the operations; the second sponsor's skill is growth — new geographies, new products, a digital channel the first owner never built. Each fund does what it is good at, and the company benefits from both in sequence. When this is true, the SBO is not pass-the-parcel; it is a relay race. The research finds that SBOs where buyer and seller have complementary skill sets genuinely outperform other buyouts (Degeorge, Martin & Phalippou, 2016).

**The less respectable reason: pressure to deploy.** A fund that has raised money must invest it — LPs pay management fees on committed capital, and a fund that sits on cash struggles to raise the next one. A fund late in its investment period, with dry powder still unspent, is under pressure to do deals, and an SBO is the easiest deal to do quickly: the asset is known, the auction is running, the financing is pre-arranged. The buyer in this situation is not buying because the company is a great investment at this price. It is buying because it needs to buy *something*.

## 3. How an SBO Differs from a Primary Buyout

The mechanics of the transaction are mostly the same as a primary LBO — the same sources and uses, the same debt stack, the same auction process from article 11. But four features are specific to the secondary deal, and a banker working on one needs to know them.

**The auction is faster, and the information is better.** In a primary buyout of a family business, the buyer spends months learning the company. In an SBO, the seller hands over a data room that another PE firm already built: audited financials, a quality-of-earnings report, monthly management accounts, a working capital analysis. Management has already been through a sale process once and knows how to answer diligence questions. The whole process compresses — and because the seller is a sophisticated party running a tight auction, the price discovery is usually sharper. There are fewer bargains in SBO auctions than in primary deals.

**Staple financing.** The seller's bankers often arrange a financing package in advance — a "staple" — and offer it to every bidder on the same terms. It removes financing uncertainty and supports the price, which is good for the seller. It is worth remembering who it is *not* necessarily good for: a buyer who accepts the staple without testing whether cheaper debt is available elsewhere may be paying for the seller's convenience.

**Management gets a second bite.** The management team usually rolls over part of its equity into the new deal. This is often presented as alignment, and it is, partly. But it also means management has now been paid twice for the same company: once at the first exit, once at the second. The second bite is a powerful retention tool, and it is one reason management teams rarely resist an SBO, even when the new leverage is aggressive.

**The price is set by the debt market, not by a strategist.** In a strategic sale, the buyer can pay more because of synergies — cost savings, revenue combinations, a distribution network the target lacks. In an SBO, there are no synergies: the buyer is a financial owner, just like the seller. The maximum price is therefore set by how much debt the market will lend against the company's cash flows, plus the equity return the buyer is willing to accept. When debt is cheap and plentiful, SBO prices rise — not because the company got better, but because the financing got easier. This is why SBO volumes and prices track the credit cycle so closely, and why they cluster in the same hot credit markets as dividend recaps.

There is also a structural fact worth stating plainly: **the same company, bought twice, pays fees twice.** Two sets of transaction fees, and — for an LP invested in both the selling fund and the buying fund — two layers of management fee and carried interest on the same underlying business. This is the most common objection to SBOs, and it sounds damning. The research tests it directly: Degeorge, Martin & Phalippou (2016) find that investors do *not* pay higher total transaction costs as a result of SBOs, even when they hold stakes in both funds. The fee objection is intuitive, but the evidence does not support it. The performance objection, on the other hand, does hold up — for a specific subset of deals.

## 4. The Worked Example: Meridian Packaging

Let us put numbers on the core problem: the second buyer pays more for the same company, and has to work harder to earn the same return. **Meridian Packaging** is a fictional maker of industrial packaging. All numbers are in millions of dollars.

**Fund A buys Meridian (Year 0).** EBITDA is $40 million, and Fund A pays 10.0x EBITDA — an enterprise value of $400 million. The debt is 6.0x EBITDA, and the rest is equity.

```math
Entry EV = 10.0 × $40m = $400m
Debt at entry = 6.0 × $40m = $240m
Sponsor equity = $400m − $240m = $160m
```

**Five years later (Year 5).** Fund A has done its work — cost discipline, a small add-on acquisition, better pricing. EBITDA has grown to $60 million, a 50% increase, or about 8.4% a year. The company has paid down $40 million of debt, so debt is now $200 million. Fund A's fund is in year eight of its life; it needs to exit. The IPO window is shut. No strategist bids. A second sponsor, Fund B, wins the auction — at 11.0x EBITDA, one turn higher than Fund A paid.

```math
Exit EV for Fund A = 11.0 × $60m = $660m
Equity value at exit = $660m − $200m = $460m
Fund A MOIC = $460m / $160m = 2.88x
Fund A IRR = 2.88^(1/5) − 1 ≈ 23.5%
```

Fund A did well: 2.88x its money, a 23.5% IRR. Part of that came from EBITDA growth, part from the multiple expanding from 10.0x to 11.0x — the market was willing to pay more per dollar of earnings at exit than at entry — and part from leverage: the debt stayed roughly flat while the equity value grew. That multiple expansion is a gift from the credit cycle, not from the operations, and Fund A is happy to take it.

**Fund B buys Meridian (Year 5, now Year 0 for the second deal).** Same company, same EBITDA of $60 million — but Fund B pays $660 million, and it must fund that price with new debt and new equity. It borrows the same 6.0x EBITDA.

```math
Entry EV for Fund B = $660m  (11.0 × $60m)
Debt at entry = 6.0 × $60m = $360m
Sponsor equity = $660m − $360m = $300m
```

Notice the equity cheque: $300 million, nearly double Fund A's $160 million, for the same company. That is the arithmetic of buying at a higher multiple. And the interest bill is heavier too: at an 8% rate, Fund B pays about $29 million a year in interest, against $60 million of EBITDA — coverage of about 2.1x, the same as Fund A's entry coverage, but on a much larger absolute debt stack.

**Fund B's next five years — the same operating performance.** Suppose Fund B does exactly as well as Fund A did: EBITDA grows 50% over five years, to $90 million, and the exit multiple is again 11.0x. The company pays down $20 million of debt a year, so debt at exit is $260 million.

```math
Exit EV for Fund B = 11.0 × $90m = $990m
Equity value at exit = $990m − $260m = $730m
Fund B MOIC = $730m / $300m = 2.43x
Fund B IRR = 2.43^(1/5) − 1 ≈ 19.5%
```

Same growth rate, same exit multiple, same leverage discipline — and Fund B earns 2.43x and 19.5%, against Fund A's 2.88x and 23.5%. The four percentage points of IRR did not go to worse operations. They went to the entry price: Fund B paid 11.0x where Fund A paid 10.0x, and the bigger equity cheque diluted the benefit of the leverage. This is the central arithmetic of the secondary buyout, and it is worth memorising: **every turn of entry multiple the second buyer pays is a turn of return it must earn back through operations.**

**What would Fund B need to match Fund A?** To earn the same 2.88x MOIC, Fund B's exit equity must be 2.88 × $300m = $862.5 million. With $260 million of debt at exit, that means an enterprise value of $1,122.5 million — and at an 11.0x exit multiple, EBITDA of about $102 million. That is 70% growth over five years, or about 11.2% a year, against the 8.4% a year Fund A achieved. The second sponsor must grow the company roughly a third faster than the first one did, just to earn the same return — on a company that has already had its easiest improvements made. If the first sponsor's playbook is genuinely finished, where is that extra growth supposed to come from? That is the question a banker should ask, out loud, before recommending an SBO to a buyer.

## 5. What the Research Says

Three papers, published between 2012 and 2016, form the core evidence on SBOs. They agree on the facts and disagree — productively — on how damning those facts are.

**Wang (2012)** asks the most basic question: why do SBOs happen at all? Yingdi Wang, *"Secondary buyouts: Why buy and at what price?"*, Journal of Corporate Finance 18(5): 1306–1325, tests three explanations — efficiency gains, liquidity-based market timing, and collusion — and finds the evidence points to market timing. SBOs are more likely when the equity market is "cold" (so IPOs are unattractive), when the debt market is favourable (so a leveraged buyer can pay up), and when the seller faces a high demand for liquidity. She finds no strong efficiency gains for target firms, and no evidence of collusion. SBOs are also priced higher than first-time buyouts — but that premium is driven by favourable debt conditions, not by the companies being better. Her conclusion, in plain terms: SBOs mostly exist to solve the *seller's* liquidity problem, not to create value in the company.

**Arcot, Fluck, Gaspar & Hege (2015)** go after the incentive problem directly. *"Fund managers under pressure: Rationale and determinants of secondary buyouts"*, Journal of Financial Economics 115(1): 102–135, builds buy-pressure and sell-pressure indexes — how close a fund is to the end of its investment period or its life, how much capital it still has unspent, its reputation, its deal activity — and applies them to 9,771 LBO deals in the US and twelve European countries from 1980 to 2010. The findings are blunt: funds under pressure engage in more SBOs. Pressured *buyers* pay higher multiples, use less leverage, and syndicate less — the signature of a buyer whose motive is to spend equity, not to find a bargain. Pressured *sellers* exit at lower multiples and accept shorter holding periods. And funds that invested under pressure underperform.

**Degeorge, Martin & Phalippou (2016)** give the most nuanced answer, and it is the one to remember. *"On secondary buyouts"*, Journal of Financial Economics 120(1): 124–145, uses a sample of 5,849 buyouts with precise returns data. Their findings split the market in two. SBOs made *late* in the buying fund's investment period — when the fund is under pressure to spend — underperform other buyouts by a wide margin: the public market equivalent (PME) is about 0.3 lower, and late SBOs generate negative net present value for LPs, returning about $0.88 for every $1 a stock market investment would have returned. But SBOs made *early* in the investment period, which are nearly two-thirds of the sample, perform as well as other buyouts and generate positive NPV. And when buyer and seller have complementary skill sets, SBOs *outperform* other buyouts. Two more findings deserve emphasis. First, investors punish the behaviour: LPs reduce their capital allocations to firms that do pressured SBOs. Second, as noted in section 3, LPs do not pay higher total transaction costs from SBOs, even when they hold stakes in both funds.

Read together, the three papers tell a coherent story. SBOs are not inherently value-destroying — the "pass the parcel" charge, stated absolutely, is wrong. They are a legitimate exit when the equity market is cold, a legitimate entry when the buyer's skills genuinely complement the seller's, and a legitimate way to keep a good company in professional hands when an IPO is not available. But they are also the transaction type most exposed to the agency problem at the heart of private equity: the fund manager's incentives — to deploy capital before the investment period ends, to realise gains before fundraising, to show DPI — can diverge from the LP's interest in returns. When those incentives drive the deal, the buyer overpays, the returns fall short, and the LP pays for it. The difference between a good SBO and a bad one is not the structure. It is the motive. And the motive is usually visible in the calendar: a buyer late in its investment period, or a seller late in its fund life, should make everyone in the room more careful.

## 6. The Famous Example: Springer, Three Sponsors in Six Years

No company illustrates the SBO relay race better than Springer Science+Business Media, the German academic publisher. In 2009, Cinven and Candover — the private equity firms that owned Springer — sold it to two other private equity firms, EQT and the Government of Singapore Investment Corporation, in a deal confirmed in February 2010 after competition authorities in the US and Europe approved the transfer. Four years later, in 2013, EQT and GIC sold a majority stake to a third private equity firm, BC Partners, for $4.4 billion. And in January 2015, Springer announced a merger with Holtzbrinck Publishing Group's Nature Publishing Group; the transaction closed that May, forming Springer Nature, with Holtzbrinck holding 53% and BC Partners retaining 47%.

Three sets of private equity owners in six years, and the company never once went public or sold to a strategist in that period. Each sponsor presumably had its own thesis, and the final step — the merger with Nature — was the kind of strategic combination that only made sense once the company had been through several rounds of financial ownership. Springer is also a reminder of the limits of the SBO story: the academic publishing business kept generating cash through all of it, which is what made the repeated leverage possible. A cyclical business would not have survived three successive leveraged owners.

## 7. Judging an SBO: A Checklist

An SBO is a judgement call, and the judgement depends on whose seat you are sitting in. A banker advising on one should be able to answer the questions for each party.

**For the buyer — three questions.**

**1. What can we do that the seller could not?** Name the specific skill, and name the evidence for it. "International expansion" is not a skill; "we took our last three portfolio companies into Southeast Asia and grew revenue 30% a year doing it" is. If the honest answer is "nothing, really — it is a good company and we need to deploy capital," the deal should not happen. Complementary skills are the only version of the SBO that the research finds outperforms, and the burden of proof is on the buyer.

**2. What growth rate does the entry price require — and is it credible?** Run the arithmetic from section 4. Compute the EBITDA growth needed to earn the fund's target return at the price being paid. Then compare it with the growth the *seller* achieved, and ask whether the remaining opportunities are bigger or smaller than the ones already taken. If the required growth is faster than the seller's — on a company that has already been improved once — the price is too high, however good the business looks.

**3. Are we buying because of the calendar?** Check where the fund is in its investment period. If the deal is being pushed because the investment period is ending and there is dry powder left, say so explicitly — to the investment committee, at least. Arcot et al. (2015) and Degeorge et al. (2016) both find that pressured buyers overpay and underperform. A fund that names the pressure can still do the deal, but it cannot pretend the pressure is not there.

**For the seller — two questions.**

**1. Is this the best exit available, or the only one left?** An SBO is a good exit when the IPO window is shut and no strategist is bidding. It is a bad exit when it is chosen because it is fast and certain while a better-priced IPO or strategic sale is available in six months. The seller's fund-life clock is real, but it is not the company's problem — and LPs can tell the difference between a well-timed exit and a forced one.

**2. What are we leaving on the table?** The seller that exits at 11.0x is handing the buyer a company it believes can support 11.0x. If the seller's own analysis says the company is worth more — if there is a credible plan the seller simply does not have time to execute — the honest move is to say so, and to consider whether a partial exit, a continuation vehicle, or a longer hold serves the LPs better. (Continuation funds are the subject of the next article.)

**For the LP — one question.**

**1. Am I paying twice for the same company?** If you hold stakes in both the selling fund and the buying fund, you are, in economic terms, selling the company to yourself and paying two sets of fees for the privilege. The research says the total transaction costs are not higher — but the *performance* question remains: is the second fund going to earn an acceptable return on a company bought at a higher multiple than the first fund paid? The LP's protection is not a rule against SBOs. It is the allocation decision: funds that do pressured SBOs get less capital next time, and the evidence says LPs do, in fact, punish them.

## 8. Common Misconceptions

**"An SBO means the first sponsor failed."** No. A sponsor can do an excellent job, double the company's value, and still sell to another sponsor — because its fund is ending, or because the next stage of growth needs different skills. The sale is not an admission of failure. It is often the *reward* for success: the company is good enough that another professional buyer wants it.

**"The second buyer must be a fool to pay more than the first."** Not necessarily. The second buyer is paying for a different company than the first one bought: larger, more profitable, professionally managed, with audited financials and a proven management team. A higher multiple on a better company can be a fair price. The fool is the buyer who pays the higher multiple for the *same* company and assumes the same returns will follow. Section 4 shows why that assumption fails.

**"SBOs are just pass-the-parcel and always destroy value."** The research rejects the absolute version of this claim. Early SBOs perform as well as other buyouts; SBOs with complementary skills outperform. What destroys value is not the structure but the motive: buying under pressure to deploy, late in the investment period. The misconception is to condemn the transaction type instead of the incentive behind the specific deal.

**"SBOs are bad for LPs because they pay fees twice."** Intuitive, and — on the evidence — wrong as a claim about total transaction costs. Degeorge, Martin & Phalippou (2016) find no higher total transaction costs for LPs with stakes in both funds. The real LP cost of a bad SBO is not the fee; it is the underperformance of a deal done under pressure. Fees are visible and easy to complain about; a 0.3 lower PME is harder to see, and far more expensive.

**"If a company is sold three times, something must be wrong with it."** Springer was sold three times in six years, and nothing was wrong with it — it was a stable, cash-generative business that successive owners kept finding valuable. Repeated SBOs are a sign that the company generates enough cash to support repeated leverage, which is a compliment, not an indictment. The warning sign is not repetition; it is rising leverage on flat earnings, or a new buyer whose thesis is thinner than the last one's.

## 9. Test Yourself

**1.** Meridian Packaging: Fund A buys at 10.0x EBITDA of $40m, with debt of 6.0x EBITDA. Five years later EBITDA is $60m, debt has been paid down to $200m, and Fund B buys the company at 11.0x EBITDA. What is Fund A's MOIC and IRR? What equity cheque does Fund B write, and what is its debt at entry?

**2.** Fund B grows EBITDA at the same rate Fund A did (50% over five years, to $90m) and exits at 11.0x, with debt paid down to $260m. Compute Fund B's MOIC and IRR, and explain in one paragraph why they are lower than Fund A's even though the operating performance was identical.

**3.** A fund is in the final year of its investment period with $400 million of dry powder still unspent. It is bidding for a company in an SBO auction at a multiple one turn above the seller's entry multiple. Using Arcot et al. (2015) and Degeorge et al. (2016), name three reasons the investment committee should be sceptical of this deal.

**4.** Wang (2012) finds that SBOs are more likely when the equity market is "cold", the debt market is favourable, and the seller needs liquidity — and that SBOs are priced higher than primary buyouts because of debt market conditions. What does this imply about *when* SBO prices are highest, and what should a buyer conclude from a high SBO price about the quality of the company?
