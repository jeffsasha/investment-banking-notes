# 42 | Continuation Funds: When a Sponsor Sells to Itself

*Article 41 ended with a sponsor selling a good company to another sponsor because the fund's clock had run out. But there is a fourth exit that article 41 only hinted at — one that did not exist in any serious form twenty years ago and is now, by volume, the largest single exit route in private equity. The sponsor does not sell the company to a strategist, to the public, or to another sponsor. It sells the company to itself. A new fund, run by the same manager, buys the asset out of the old fund, the old fund's investors get a choice between cash and rolling into the new vehicle, and the manager keeps running the company for another five years — with the fee clock and the carry clock both reset to zero. This is the continuation fund. It is the most useful innovation in private equity in a generation, and the most conflicted transaction a fund manager will ever run. This article is about both halves of that sentence.*

> **Learning goals.** After this article you can: (1) define a continuation fund (CV) and explain how it differs from a secondary buyout, an LP-led secondary sale, and a straight fund extension; (2) explain why a GP would rather move an asset into a CV than sell it, including the fund-life clock and the exit drought; (3) walk through the mechanics — the advisor-run process, the price-setting, and the roll-versus-cash election; (4) compute how a CV crystallises carry and resets fees, and show the arithmetic for both the GP and a rolling LP; (5) describe the conflict of interest at the centre of the deal and the safeguards the industry has built around it — the ILPA guidance, the LPAC vote, and the SEC's reporting rule; (6) judge a proposed CV from the point of view of an LP deciding whether to roll or take cash.
>
> **Prerequisites.** Article 08 (LBO) for sources and uses, MOIC, IRR, and carry; article 11 (sell-side M&A) for how an auction sets a price; article 39 (take-privates) for the primary buyout as the starting point; article 41 (secondary buyouts) for the fund-life clock and the SBO as the alternative exit — the CV is best understood as the SBO's in-house cousin.

## 1. The Idea in One Paragraph

A **continuation fund** — a **CV**, one type of **GP-led secondary transaction** — is a new fund created by a private equity manager (the **GP**, or general partner) to buy one or more portfolio companies out of an older fund that the same GP manages. The old fund is nearing the end of its life and has to return capital. Instead of selling the company to an outside buyer, the GP sets up a fresh vehicle, raises new money — partly from the old fund's existing investors, who may **roll** their exposure into the new fund, and partly from new secondary investors — and the new vehicle buys the company at a price set through a process run by an outside advisor. The company never changes manager. The investors who want out get cash; the investors who believe in the asset stay in; the GP keeps managing it, and the clock on management fees and carried interest starts again.

That is the whole transaction, and it is worth pausing on how strange it is. In every other sale, the buyer and the seller are different people with opposite interests, and the price is the thing they argue about. In a continuation fund, the manager sits on both sides of the table at once: it is the seller (acting for the old fund), the buyer (acting for the new fund), and the party that benefits from the price, because the price decides both what the old fund distributes and how much carry the manager banks. The deal is, in the words of the industry's own guidance, "conflicted by nature". Everything interesting about continuation funds — the growth, the controversy, the regulation, the safeguards — flows from that single fact.

The scale is what makes the question unavoidable. GP-led secondary transactions reached $71 billion in 2024, about 44% of the entire $160 billion secondary market, according to Evercore's full-year 2024 review — and continuation funds were roughly 84% of that GP-led volume. A decade ago, CVs were a rescue tool for troubled funds. Today they are a mainstream portfolio-management device, used by the largest managers in the world for their best assets. The single largest deal on record, Vista Equity Partners' $5.6 billion continuation fund for Cloud Software Group in June 2025, was not a distressed situation at all: it was a manager moving one of the biggest software positions in private equity into a new vehicle because it wanted five more years with it.

## 2. Why the Clock Forces the Question

To understand why CVs exist, start with the problem article 41 described: the fund-life clock. A private equity fund typically lives ten years, with an investment period of about five. In its final years it must sell its companies and return capital to its limited partners (LPs). That timetable made sense when exits were reliable. It is a much harder constraint now, for two reasons.

**The exit drought.** Traditional exits — IPOs and strategic sales — have been weak for several years. M&A volumes have been soft, the IPO window has opened only intermittently, and the result is an enormous overhang of unsold companies: Bain & Company estimated in 2025 that buyout funds hold roughly 29,000 unsold portfolio companies worth about $3.6 trillion. A GP with a great company in an eight-year-old fund faces a genuinely unpleasant choice: sell it now, in a thin market, at a price that reflects the market's mood rather than the company's quality — or find another way to keep it.

**The best assets are the ones that hurt most to sell.** This is the part the textbook version misses. The companies a GP most wants to keep are exactly the ones the fund-life clock forces it to sell first: the winners, the compounders, the assets with five more years of growth left in them. Selling a mediocre company at year eight is no loss. Selling your best company at year eight — because the calendar says so, not because the work is done — is the fund structure destroying value. The continuation fund is, at its best, a fix for that design flaw: a way to let a good asset outlive the fund that bought it.

There are two less respectable motives, and an honest account names them. First, **DPI and fundraising**: a CV converts an unrealised gain into a realised one, the old fund's DPI rises, and the GP goes fundraising with a better track record. Second, **fees and carry**: a CV resets both. The old fund's carry crystallises at the transfer price — the GP banks its 20% of the gain even though nothing was sold to a third party — and the new vehicle charges fresh management fees and a fresh 20% carry on future gains. Section 4 puts numbers on exactly how much that is worth. None of this makes CVs illegitimate. It makes the *motive* the thing to interrogate — just as article 41 concluded about SBOs.

## 3. How a Continuation Fund Actually Works

A CV is not a quiet internal transfer. Done properly, it is run like a sale process, with the same advisors, the same data room, and the same discipline as the auction in article 11 — because the price is the one thing the GP cannot be trusted to set alone.

**Step 1: pick the asset (or assets).** Most CVs today are **single-asset** vehicles — one company, moved on its own. Single-asset CVs were about 58% of continuation funds in 2024. **Multi-asset** CVs move a small portfolio, usually two to five companies, which diversifies the new vehicle but makes the pricing harder: each asset needs its own valuation. The asset chosen is almost always a winner — a company the GP believes has years of growth left, not a problem child. (That is a real change from the early days, when CVs were mostly used to extend the life of troubled funds. The modern CV is a vehicle for the best assets, which is precisely why the conflicts matter more now, not less.)

**Step 2: run a process to set the price.** The GP hires an advisor — Evercore, Jefferies, and Lazard dominate this work — to run what looks like an auction. Third-party buyers are invited to bid on the asset. Their bids do two jobs at once: they give the old fund's LPs a genuine market price for their interests, and they give the GP a defensible transfer price. The highest credible third-party bid typically becomes the price at which the asset moves into the CV — or, if the GP's own vehicle bids higher, the price at which it wins. This is the single most important safeguard in the whole structure, and the industry's guidance insists on it: a competitive process with third-party price validation.

**Step 3: the election.** Every LP in the old fund is offered the same choice: **take cash** at the transfer price, **roll** your exposure into the new continuation vehicle, or do a bit of both. New secondary investors — specialist funds such as Coller Capital and Goldman Sachs Asset Management, who led the new money in the Vista deal — commit capital alongside the rollers, and their money funds the cash-outs. The CV also usually raises fresh debt against the company, just as a buyout would, and that debt partly funds the purchase price. The structure is, in substance, a leveraged buyout in which the buyer happens to be the seller's own manager.

**Step 4: the new fund runs the asset.** The CV has its own life — typically five years, sometimes longer — its own fee schedule, its own carry, and its own reporting. The company gets fresh capital for growth, often for add-on acquisitions or investment the old fund could no longer fund. The GP manages it exactly as it managed it before, now with a longer runway.

Three features of the structure deserve emphasis, because they are where the conflicts hide. First, **the election deadline is often short** — ILPA notes cases where LPs were given as little as ten days to decide, and recommends at least 30 calendar days. An LP that cannot complete its own investment-committee process in time is effectively forced to take cash. Second, **the default matters**: ILPA recommends that an LP which fails to submit an election should default to cash rather than being rolled involuntarily into the new vehicle. Third, **the GP's information advantage is total**. It knows the company better than any outside bidder, better than the LPAC, and better than the fairness-opinion provider. No process can fully remove that advantage. It can only be managed.

## 4. The Worked Example: Northwind Software

Let us put numbers on the two questions that matter most: what does the GP gain from the transfer, and what does a rolling LP actually earn? **Northwind Software** is a fictional maker of logistics software. All numbers are in millions of dollars.

**Fund I buys Northwind (Year 0).** EBITDA is $80 million, and Fund I pays 10.0x EBITDA — an enterprise value of $800 million. The debt is 6.0x EBITDA, and the rest is equity.

```math
Entry EV = 10.0 × $80m = $800m
Debt at entry = 6.0 × $80m = $480m
Sponsor equity = $800m − $480m = $320m
```

**Seven years later (Year 7).** Fund I has done its work — the product moved to the cloud, pricing improved, two add-on acquisitions closed. EBITDA has doubled to $160 million. The company has paid down debt to $400 million. Fund I is in year eight of its life; it must exit. The IPO window is shut, and no strategist bids. The GP proposes a continuation fund at 15.0x EBITDA.

```math
Transfer EV = 15.0 × $160m = $2,400m
Equity value at transfer = $2,400m − $400m = $2,000m
Fund I MOIC = $2,000m / $320m = 6.25x
Fund I IRR = 6.25^(1/7) − 1 ≈ 29.9%
```

Fund I did very well: 6.25x its money, a 29.9% IRR. Part of that came from EBITDA doubling, part from the multiple expanding from 10.0x to 15.0x — the market now pays far more per dollar of software earnings than it did seven years ago — and part from leverage. Now comes the part that makes the CV different from a sale.

**The carry crystallises — on a sale to itself.** Fund I's carry is 20% of the gain. Simplified, ignoring the hurdle rate and the preferred return:

```math
Gain at transfer = $2,000m − $320m = $1,680m
GP carry crystallised = 20% × $1,680m = $336m
```

The GP banks $336 million of carry at the transfer — for a transaction in which the company did not change hands to anyone outside the manager's own funds. If Fund I had simply held the company, that carry would still be unrealised, paper value. The CV converts it into a crystallised entitlement. This is not a criticism on its own: the LPs have also made 6.25x, and the carry is the agreed price of that performance. But notice what it means for the GP's incentives. The GP's immediate payday depends on the transfer price, and the GP is the party running the process that sets the transfer price. A price one turn of EBITDA higher — 16.0x instead of 15.0x, an enterprise value of $2,560 million — would add $160 million to the equity value and $32 million to the GP's carry. The direction of the temptation is obvious, and so is the reason the price-setting process needs outside bids and an LPAC vote.

**The CV funds the purchase.** The new vehicle buys the company at the $2,400 million transfer value, funding it the way any buyout is funded — new debt plus new equity:

```math
New debt = 6.0 × $160m = $960m
New equity needed = $2,400m − $960m = $1,440m
Uses: repay old debt $400m + pay Fund I $2,000m = $2,400m  ✓
```

The LPs elect: suppose 60% of the equity value rolls into the CV ($1,200 million) and 40% takes cash ($800 million). New secondary investors supply the remaining equity — $1,440 million minus $1,200 million rolled, or $240 million. The old fund distributes $800 million of cash plus the rolled interests, and winds down.

**Five years in the CV.** Suppose Northwind grows EBITDA by 50% over the next five years — the same growth rate Fund A achieved in article 41's Meridian example — to $240 million, and exits at the same 15.0x multiple, with debt paid down to $700 million.

```math
Exit EV = 15.0 × $240m = $3,600m
Equity value at exit = $3,600m − $700m = $2,900m
CV MOIC = $2,900m / $1,440m = 2.01x
CV IRR = 2.01^(1/5) − 1 ≈ 15.0%
```

A rolling LP's total outcome: take $100 of transfer value. $40 is taken as cash today; $60 rolls into the CV and grows by the CV's 2.01x multiple to about $120.83. Total proceeds are $160.83 on $100 of transfer value — a 1.61x multiple over the five CV years, or about a 10.0% IRR. Compare that with the LP who took all cash at the transfer and redeployed it elsewhere: the roller kept exposure to a company they already knew, at a price set by a process the GP controlled. Whether 10.0% is a good return depends entirely on what else that LP could have done with the money — which is the question section 8 tells them to ask.

**The GP's second payday.** The CV charges its own management fee — say 1.5% a year on the $1,440 million of committed equity, roughly $108 million over five years — and its own carry: 20% of the CV's gain, or 20% × ($2,900m − $1,440m) = $292 million. Add the $336 million crystallised at the transfer, and the GP's total economics across the two vehicles are roughly $736 million, against a single fund's carry of $336 million plus its fees. The fee clock reset is not a footnote. On a large asset, it is worth hundreds of millions of dollars, and it is paid by the same LPs who are being asked to roll.

| | Fund I (original) | Continuation fund |
|---|---:|---:|
| Entry / transfer EBITDA | $80m | $160m |
| Entry / transfer multiple | 10.0x | 15.0x |
| Entry / transfer EV | $800m | $2,400m |
| Equity | $320m | $1,440m |
| Exit EBITDA (5–7 yrs) | $160m | $240m |
| Exit EV | $2,400m | $3,600m |
| MOIC | 6.25x | 2.01x |
| IRR | ~29.9% | ~15.0% |
| GP carry | $336m crystallised | $292m + ~$108m fees |

None of this arithmetic is an argument against continuation funds. It is an argument for reading the price carefully. Everything in the deal — the LP's return, the GP's carry, the fairness of the election — runs through a single number, the transfer price, and the GP sits on both sides of it.

## 5. The Conflict at the Centre — and the Safeguards

The conflict is structural, not a matter of bad actors. The GP is the seller's agent and the buyer's agent, the price-setter and the price-taker, the party that banks carry on the transfer and the party that charges fees on the new vehicle. The industry has spent the last five years building scaffolding around that conflict, and a banker working on these deals needs to know the pieces.

**The LPAC vote.** The Institutional Limited Partners Association (ILPA) published dedicated continuation fund guidance in May 2023, following its broader GP-led secondaries paper from April 2019. Its central recommendation: the GP should present the rationale for the CV — including the alternatives it explored — to the fund's Limited Partner Advisory Committee, and the LPAC should vote on whether to waive the conflicts of interest, even when the fund documents already pre-clear them. The GP should not rely on pre-clearance language to skip the conversation. The guidance also sets two general principles that are worth memorising: the transaction should maximise value for existing LPs, and rolling LPs should be no worse off than if the transaction had not occurred.

**Third-party price validation and fairness opinions.** ILPA recommends a competitive process with third-party price validation, and that a fairness opinion from an independent provider be obtained for all GP-led transactions. The opinion is not a guarantee — a fairness opinion says the price is within a range, not that it is the best price — but it forces a second set of eyes onto the valuation, and it creates a document the LPAC can point to.

**Time to decide.** ILPA recommends LPs receive at least 30 calendar days to evaluate the proposal, and that non-responding LPs default to cash rather than being rolled in. Both recommendations exist because of real cases where LPs were given days, not weeks, and where the default quietly rolled them into a new fund they had not underwritten.

**Regulatory attention.** The US Securities and Exchange Commission has also moved. In May 2023 it adopted rules requiring private fund advisers to report adviser-led secondary transactions within 60 days after the quarter in which they occur, on the stated ground that these transactions carry a higher potential for conflicts of interest and may signal an investor-protection issue. The SEC's broader Private Fund Adviser Rule would have required a fairness or valuation opinion from an independent provider for every continuation fund — but that rule was vacated by the Fifth Circuit in 2024, so the opinion requirement remains market practice and ILPA guidance rather than law. The regulatory position, in short: the SEC is watching, it wants data, and it has not yet imposed the strongest version of the rules it proposed.

None of these safeguards removes the conflict. They manage it, the way a fairness opinion in article 10 manages the conflicts in a sale process without removing them. A CV is a transaction in which the usual protection — an arm's-length buyer who wants the lowest price — is absent, and every safeguard is a substitute for that missing party. The substitutes are better than nothing, and worse than the real thing.

## 6. What the Evidence Says

Academic research on continuation funds specifically is still thin — the product grew faster than the literature — but three strands of evidence bear on it.

**The liquidity cost of secondaries.** Nadauld, Sensoy, Vorkink & Weisbach (2019), *"The Liquidity Cost of Private Equity Investments: Evidence from Secondary Market Transactions"*, Journal of Financial Economics 132(3): 158–181, studied LP-led secondary sales from 2006 to 2014 and found that most transactions occur at a discount to net asset value — 13.8% on average, 9% for the most common type of sale (funds four to nine years old) — and that buyers outperform sellers by a market-adjusted five percentage points a year. The paper is about LP-led sales, not CVs, and its lesson cuts both ways. On the one hand, it explains why LPs like the CV's cash option: a GP-led process that prices near NAV is a much better exit than an LP-led secondary sale at a 9–14% discount. On the other hand, it is a warning about who profits from supplying liquidity: in secondaries, the buyer of liquidity earns the return, and in a CV the new secondary investors are the liquidity suppliers — the transfer price decides how much of the return they capture versus the rolling LPs.

**The market has voted with its wallet.** The volume figures in section 1 are the strongest evidence available: GP-led transactions of $71 billion in 2024 (Evercore), continuation funds at roughly 84% of that, single-asset CVs the majority, and repeat sponsors accounting for 42% of 2024 CV volume. A tool that managers use once in distress and never again is a rescue device. A tool that the same managers use repeatedly, for their best assets, is a portfolio-management strategy. But the repeat usage is also the number that should make an LP most careful: a GP that runs CVs as a regular part of its playbook has a structural incentive to prefer them over a third-party sale, and the LPAC's job is to test each one on its own merits rather than accepting the pattern.

**The SBO evidence applies by analogy.** The three papers from article 41 — Wang (2012), Arcot, Fluck, Gaspar & Hege (2015), and Degeorge, Martin & Phalippou (2016) — all studied sponsor-to-sponsor deals, and their central finding was about motive: deals done under pressure underperform, deals done early with complementary skills do fine. The same logic applies to CVs, with the pressure running in a different direction. The question is not whether the structure works — it plainly can. It is whether this GP, at this price, with this asset, is moving it because the company has five good years left, or because the fund needs a distribution and the next fund needs a track record.

## 7. The Famous Example: Vista and Cloud Software Group

No deal illustrates the modern CV better than Vista Equity Partners' continuation fund for **Cloud Software Group**, closed in June 2025 — the largest single-asset continuation fund on record, at $5.6 billion.

Cloud Software Group was itself a Vista creation: the company formed in 2022 from Vista's $16.5 billion take-private and combination of Citrix and TIBCO Software. Three years later, with the asset sitting in Vista's flagship Funds VII and VIII, Vista moved it into a continuation vehicle rather than selling it or taking it public. The new vehicle raised $2.7 billion in fresh capital from secondary investors led by Coller Capital and Goldman Sachs Asset Management, alongside about $2.2 billion rolled over from the existing funds. The transfer priced at a 5% discount to the company's first-quarter 2024 net asset value, on an enterprise value reported at roughly $26.9 billion — and existing LPs who took the cash option were reported to receive roughly a 4.1x multiple on their money.

Every feature of the modern CV is visible in that one deal. The asset was a winner, not a problem: a scaled software platform Vista had spent three years integrating. The exit alternatives were unattractive: an IPO of a $27 billion software business in a soft market, or a strategic sale to a buyer who would have to fund an enormous cheque. The pricing was set with reference to NAV and validated by third-party capital — the $2.7 billion of new money from Coller and Goldman Sachs Asset Management was, in effect, the market's vote on the price. And the scale shows how far the product has travelled: a $5.6 billion single-asset vehicle would have been unthinkable as a "secondary" transaction a decade ago. For scale, the next-largest CVs reported in 2025 — New Mountain's $3.1 billion single-asset vehicle for Real Chemistry, closed in April 2025 — were barely more than half its size.

Vista's deal is also a useful reminder of what a CV does *not* do. It does not create value by itself. The value was created in the three years Vista spent combining Citrix and TIBCO, cutting costs, and moving the product to the cloud. The CV is a financing and structuring decision about who owns the next five years of that value — and on what terms.

## 8. Judging a Continuation Fund: A Checklist

A CV is a judgement call, and the judgement depends on whose seat you are sitting in.

**For the LP deciding whether to roll or take cash — three questions.**

**1. Would I buy this company today, at this price, if I had never owned it?** This is the only question that matters, and it is the one the structure is designed to blur. Strip away the history, the sunk cost, the relationship with the GP, and the flattery of being offered a choice. The transfer price is a purchase price. If the company, at 15.0x EBITDA, is not a buy at that price on a standalone basis, the roll is not a hold — it is a new investment at a full price, made under time pressure, in a vehicle whose manager has already banked carry on it. If the answer is yes, roll. If the answer is no, take the cash.

**2. What is the alternative use of the cash?** The CV's cash option is only as good as the LP's next investment. An LP that takes cash and redeploys it into a new fund at a similar return has lost nothing. An LP that takes cash because it distrusts the process, and then sits on it, has swapped a 10% IRR for zero. The roll-versus-cash decision is a relative-value decision, not a referendum on the GP.

**3. Was the price set by a real process?** Ask whether third-party buyers actually bid, whether the highest bid set the price, whether a fairness opinion was obtained, and whether the LPAC voted on the conflicts. If the process was competitive and the price is at or near NAV with real third-party money committed — as in the Vista deal — the conflict has been managed about as well as it can be. If the process was thin, the opinion absent, or the election deadline days rather than weeks, the price deserves scepticism, and the cash option deserves a second look.

**For the GP — two questions.**

**1. Is this the best outcome for the old fund's LPs, or the best outcome for the GP?** The two usually overlap — a good asset held longer is good for both — but they diverge exactly when the price is set. A GP that can show it explored a third-party sale, ran a competitive process, and accepted a price set by outside bids has an answer. A GP that cannot has a problem, and the LPAC will ask.

**2. What happens to the LPs who roll if the next five years disappoint?** The rollers are the GP's most trusting investors. They gave up liquidity at the transfer and accepted a fresh fee and carry schedule. If the CV underperforms, the GP has still banked the $336 million of carry from the transfer. That asymmetry is the reason ILPA's second principle exists — rolling LPs should be no worse off than if the transaction had not occurred — and a GP that cannot explain how it honours that principle should not be running the deal.

**For the banker advising on the process — one question.**

**1. Is my process genuinely competitive, or is it theatre?** A process in which third-party bidders are invited but the GP's vehicle is always going to win — because the reserve price is set above what any outsider will pay, or because the diligence window is too short for a real bid — produces a price that looks market-tested and is not. The advisor's reputation is the only asset in this market that cannot be rebuilt, and a CV process is where it is most visibly on the line.

## 9. Common Misconceptions

**"A continuation fund is just a way for GPs to avoid selling."** Partly true, and the wrong conclusion to draw from it. Avoiding a sale is the point — when the sale would be badly timed or badly priced. The error is to treat "not selling" as inherently suspicious. A GP that sells a great company into a cold market because the fund agreement says so is not being disciplined; it is being mechanical. The question is never whether the GP avoided a sale. It is whether the price and the process justify the avoidance.

**"The GP is buying its own asset, so the price must be rigged."** Not necessarily, and the evidence from the largest deals points the other way. The Vista transfer priced at a 5% discount to NAV, with $2.7 billion of independent third-party capital committed at that price. A rigged price would not attract Coller Capital and Goldman Sachs Asset Management. The conflict is real, but a competitive process with real outside money is a genuine mitigant — which is why the quality of the process, not the existence of the conflict, is what an LP should investigate.

**"Rolling into the CV is just holding — nothing changes."** Everything changes. The roll is a new investment: new fund, new fees, new carry, new five-year clock, new debt stack, and a new valuation as the entry price. An LP that treats the roll as a passive hold has not understood the transaction. Section 4 shows the difference in plain numbers: the rolling LP's $100 of transfer value becomes a $40 cash payment plus a $60 investment in a new fund earning about 10% a year — a completely different risk from simply continuing to own the asset in the old fund.

**"Continuation funds are for troubled assets."** That was true of the early GP-led restructurings, and it is the opposite of the modern market. Today's CVs are overwhelmingly for the best assets — the winners a GP wants to keep. That inversion matters for diligence: the assets in a CV are usually high quality, which makes the price the argument, not the asset. A banker who walks into a CV process expecting distressed debt will misread the deal entirely.

**"If the LPs wanted to stay invested, they would just extend the fund."** A fund extension — amending the fund agreement to add two or three years — is a real alternative, and it is sometimes the right one. But an extension keeps every LP locked in together, including the ones who need liquidity, and it does not reset the fee and carry economics or bring in fresh capital for growth. The CV's advantage over an extension is precisely that it sorts LPs by preference: those who want out get out, those who want to stay stay, and new money comes in. Its disadvantage is that the sorting happens at a price the GP helped set, and an extension has no price at all. When the LPAC debates the two options, that trade-off is the debate.

**3.** Exit EV = 15.0 × $240m = **$3,600m**; exit equity = $3,600m − $700m = **$2,900m**. CV MOIC = $2,900m / $1,440m = **2.01x**. CV IRR = 2.01^(1/5) − 1 ≈ **15.0%**. The LP's rolled $60 grows to $60 × 2.0139 ≈ **$120.83**; total proceeds = $40 + $120.83 = **$160.83** on $100 of transfer value — a 1.61x multiple over five years, or an IRR of 1.6083^(1/5) − 1 ≈ **10.0%**. Note the roll's return (10.0%) is lower than the CV's headline IRR (15.0%) because 40% of the LP's value sat in cash earning nothing from the transfer date.

**4.** Four problems: **(a)** no competitive process — ILPA recommends a competitive process with third-party price validation; the LPAC should require outside bids, with the highest credible bid setting (or disciplining) the transfer price. **(b)** no fairness opinion — ILPA recommends an independent fairness or valuation opinion for all GP-led transactions; the LPAC should require one from a provider with no material business relationship with the GP. **(c)** ten days to elect — ILPA recommends at least 30 calendar days, because LPs must re-underwrite the asset as a new investment through their own investment committees; the LPAC should insist on a longer window. **(d)** the GP is on both sides with no LPAC conflict waiver — ILPA recommends the GP present the rationale and alternatives to the LPAC and that the LPAC vote to waive the conflicts; the SEC's May 2023 rule also requires the transaction to be reported within 60 days after the quarter, reflecting its view that adviser-led secondaries carry heightened conflict risk. The LPAC should demand the rationale, the alternatives explored, and a formal vote — and the default for non-responding LPs should be cash, not an involuntary roll.
