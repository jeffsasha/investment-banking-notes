# 24 | Private Credit: How Non-Bank Lenders Took Over the Leveraged Loan Market

*For most of modern finance, if a company wanted to borrow a few hundred million dollars, it went to a bank — or to a syndicate of banks and institutional investors arranged by a bank. Then, quietly and then very quickly, a different kind of lender showed up: a fund, not a bank, that would lend the whole amount itself, in weeks rather than months, and hold the loan to maturity. This article is about that lender, why borrowers pay extra for it, why investors love it, and what could go wrong in a market that has never been tested at its current size.*

> **Learning goals.** After this article you can: (1) explain what private credit is and how a direct loan differs from a syndicated loan; (2) describe a unitranche facility and compare its cost against a first-lien/second-lien structure, with numbers; (3) explain why borrowers accept a higher rate and why lenders accept illiquidity, using the concepts of certainty, speed, OID, and PIK; (4) name the four risks that worry regulators about private credit, and say which of them are genuinely new; (5) explain what a BDC is and why the fund structure matters as much as the loan itself.
>
> **Prerequisites.** Article 06 (DCF I) for the cost-of-debt intuition, article 08 (LBO) for how leverage is sized as a multiple of EBITDA, and article 20 (debt capital markets) for how syndicated loans and bonds are sold. No new valuation model is introduced here; the math is loan pricing, coverage ratios, and lender returns.

## 1. The Loan That Disappeared

Imagine you are the CFO of a mid-sized software company. Your private equity owner wants to buy out a competitor, and you need $480 million of debt, fast, before another bidder moves. Twenty years ago, your bankers would have launched a syndicated loan: a first-lien term loan sold to dozens of investors, plus maybe a second-lien tranche, each with its own documents, its own lender group, and its own vote. The process took months, and until the very end you did not know for sure whether it would clear — or at what price.

Today, a plausible alternative is a single phone call. A direct lending fund — run by an asset manager, not a bank — offers to lend you the entire $480 million itself, on one set of documents, with a signed commitment in a couple of weeks. The rate is higher than the syndicated route. You take it anyway.

That fund is **private credit**: lending by non-bank financial institutions, mostly asset managers, directly to companies that do not borrow in the public bond market. The loans are privately negotiated, not traded, and the lender typically holds them until they are repaid. The International Monetary Fund put the global market at more than $2 trillion in its April 2024 Global Financial Stability Report, about three-quarters of it in the United States, where private credit had grown to roughly 7 percent of all lending to non-financial firms. That is no longer a niche. It is a parallel banking system, built in about fifteen years.

How did that happen? The short answer is regulation plus demand. After the 2008 crisis, capital rules made it more expensive for banks to hold leveraged loans, especially to riskier, mid-sized borrowers. Banks stepped back. At the same time, pension funds and insurers were hungry for yield in a low-rate world, and a floating-rate loan paying 9 or 10 percent looked a lot better than a government bond paying 2. Asset managers connected the two sides: they raised closed-end funds from those investors and lent the money directly. The academic evidence fits the story. Chernenko, Erel, and Prilmeier, studying hand-collected middle-market loans from 2010 to 2015, found that about a third of loans to such firms already came directly from non-bank lenders, that non-bank borrowers were smaller, more leveraged, and more likely to have negative EBITDA, and that non-bank loans carried roughly 190 basis points higher interest rates after controlling for borrower and loan characteristics. In other words, private credit did not win by being cheaper. It won by lending where banks would not, and by being faster and more certain where banks would.

## 2. What Private Credit Actually Is

The term covers several things, and it helps to keep them separate.

**Direct lending** is the core: a fund negotiates a loan with a borrower bilaterally, or with a small "club" of two or three funds, and holds it. The borrower is usually a mid-sized company — too small or too leveraged for the public bond market, often owned by a private equity sponsor. Loan sizes run from tens of millions to, increasingly, several billion dollars for the largest deals.

**A unitranche facility** is private credit's signature product, and it deserves a careful definition because it is easy to misunderstand. In a traditional leveraged financing, debt is split into tranches by seniority: a first-lien loan gets paid first, a second-lien loan gets paid second, and each has its own interest rate and lender group. A unitranche loan collapses that stack into a single facility with a single interest rate and a single set of documents. Behind the scenes, the lenders may still split the economics — one lender may hold a "first-out" piece that gets repaid first and another a "last-out" piece that takes more risk for more return — but the borrower sees one loan, one rate, one lender group to negotiate with. The borrower pays for that simplicity, as the worked example below shows.

**The fund structure matters as much as the loan.** Most private credit funds are closed-end: investors commit capital for eight to ten years, the manager "calls" it gradually as loans are made, and investors cannot redeem on demand. That illiquidity is a feature, not a bug — it is what lets the fund hold an untradeable loan without worrying about a run. A **business development company (BDC)** is the listed cousin: a fund that lends mainly to mid-sized companies and trades on a stock exchange, giving retail investors access but exposing the fund's share price to market sentiment even though its loans do not trade. More recently, "semi-liquid" funds have offered periodic redemptions, which reintroduces exactly the liquidity mismatch the closed-end structure was designed to avoid — a point the IMF flagged, and one to watch.

Two pricing terms will recur throughout this article, so let us define them now. **OID (original issue discount)** is an upfront fee expressed as points: a loan issued at 98 with 2 points of OID means the borrower receives $98 for every $100 of face value but owes $100. The lender's return is therefore higher than the headline coupon, because it paid less than face. **PIK (payment-in-kind)** interest is interest paid not in cash but in additional debt: instead of sending the lender cash, the borrower adds the interest to the loan balance. PIK keeps a struggling borrower alive, but the debt grows while the business shrinks — a trade the lender only accepts when it believes the business will recover.

## 3. Why Borrowers Pay More: Speed, Certainty, and One Throat to Choke

The puzzle of private credit is why any rational CFO pays a higher rate for it. The answer has four parts, and only the first is about price.

First, **certainty of execution**. A syndicated loan is a public auction in miniature: the arranger "launches" it, investors indicate interest, and the price can flex up if demand is weak — or the deal can fail to clear at all. A direct lender gives a signed commitment at an agreed price. For a sponsor racing to close an acquisition, that certainty is worth real money: a failed financing can kill a deal that took a year to negotiate.

Second, **speed**. A direct loan can be documented in weeks, because one lender (or a small club) negotiates one credit agreement. A syndicated deal needs a lender presentation, a marketing period, and allocation across dozens of accounts. When a seller wants to close by quarter-end, weeks matter.

Third, **confidentiality and flexibility**. A private loan is not rated, not traded, and not quoted in the press. If the borrower later needs to amend a covenant or raise an incremental loan, it negotiates with a handful of lenders who already know the business, rather than rounding up a syndicate. Lenders, for their part, often accept looser terms on other dimensions — larger baskets for acquisitions, for example — because they hold the whole loan and can monitor it directly.

Fourth, and least flattering, **access**. Some borrowers simply cannot get a syndicated loan at any price their deal can bear: they are too small, too leveraged, or too unusual. For them, private credit is not the expensive option. It is the only option. That is the Chernenko, Erel, and Prilmeier finding in plain language: firms borrow from non-banks mainly for access, not for price.

There is a trade-off the borrower accepts knowingly. A private loan is harder to refinance — there is no liquid secondary market to sell into — and the lender, precisely because it holds the whole loan, has real power in a restructuring. "One throat to choke" cuts both ways: one lender to negotiate with in good times is also one lender whose consent you need in bad times.

## 4. Worked Example: One Loan Versus Two

Let us put numbers on the choice, using the LBO sizing language from article 08. Meet MidCo, a software company being acquired:

- EBITDA: $80 million
- Purchase price: 12.0x EBITDA = $960 million
- Debt: 6.0x EBITDA = $480 million; equity: the other $480 million
- SOFR (the floating-rate benchmark, replacing LIBOR): 4.30%, with a SOFR floor of 4.00% — the floor is not binding here, since 4.30% is above it, but if SOFR fell below 4.00%, the loan rate would be calculated off 4.00% instead

**Option A: the syndicated stack.** A first-lien term loan of $360 million (4.5x EBITDA) priced at SOFR + 425 basis points, plus a second-lien loan of $120 million (1.5x) at SOFR + 800 basis points.

```math
\text{First-lien rate} = 4.30\% + 4.25\% = 8.55\%, \quad \text{interest} = \$360\text{m} \times 8.55\% = \$30.78\text{m}
```

```math
\text{Second-lien rate} = 4.30\% + 8.00\% = 12.30\%, \quad \text{interest} = \$120\text{m} \times 12.30\% = \$14.76\text{m}
```

```math
\text{Total interest} = \$30.78\text{m} + \$14.76\text{m} = \$45.54\text{m}, \quad \text{blended rate} = \frac{\$45.54\text{m}}{\$480\text{m}} = 9.49\%
```

```math
\text{Interest coverage} = \frac{\text{EBITDA}}{\text{interest}} = \frac{\$80\text{m}}{\$45.54\text{m}} = 1.76\text{x}
```

**Option B: the unitranche.** A single $480 million facility priced at SOFR + 550 basis points.

```math
\text{Unitranche rate} = 4.30\% + 5.50\% = 9.80\%, \quad \text{interest} = \$480\text{m} \times 9.80\% = \$47.04\text{m}
```

```math
\text{Interest coverage} = \frac{\$80\text{m}}{\$47.04\text{m}} = 1.70\text{x}
```

The unitranche costs $1.50 million more per year — 31 basis points on the blended rate — and coverage slips from 1.76x to 1.70x. That is the price of simplicity, and most sponsors pay it gladly for the certainty and speed of section 3. Note what the comparison hides, though: the syndicated stack also carries two sets of fees, two lender groups to coordinate, and syndication risk. The unitranche has one set of documents and a committed lender. Apples to apples, the gap is smaller than 31 basis points suggest.

Now look at the trade from the lender's side. Suppose the unitranche carries 2 points of OID: the lender funds $470.4 million for $480 million of face value, a $9.6 million upfront discount. If the loan is held five years to maturity, that discount amortises to roughly $1.92 million a year, or 0.40 percentage points on the $480 million face:

```math
\text{All-in lender yield} \approx 9.80\% + 0.40\% = 10.20\%
```

before the lender's own fees and any losses. That is the arithmetic behind the pitch to pension funds: a floating-rate, senior, secured return in the low double digits, with the rate rising automatically if central banks hike. The pitch is honest as far as it goes. The question is what it leaves out, which is the subject of the next two sections.

## 5. When Things Go Wrong: The PIK Temptation

Suppose two years in, MidCo's EBITDA falls 25%, from $80 million to $60 million — a lost contract, a churn problem, nothing exotic. The leverage ratio jumps:

```math
\text{Leverage} = \frac{\$480\text{m}}{\$60\text{m}} = 8.0\text{x}
```

and coverage on the unitranche falls to:

```math
\frac{\$60\text{m}}{\$47.04\text{m}} = 1.28\text{x}
```

At 1.28x, the company is not yet insolvent — it still covers its interest — but it has no margin left for a bad quarter, and a traditional lender would be demanding a paydown or a restructuring conversation. A private credit lender has a third option it often prefers: amend and extend. It might agree to let part of the interest be paid in kind. Say 2.00 percentage points of the 9.80% coupon switches to PIK for a year. Cash interest falls to 7.80%:

```math
\text{Cash interest} = \$480\text{m} \times 7.80\% = \$37.44\text{m}, \quad \text{cash coverage} = \frac{\$60\text{m}}{\$37.44\text{m}} = 1.60\text{x}
```

Coverage looks healthy again. But the unpaid $9.6 million is added to the loan balance, which grows to $489.6 million, and next year's interest is charged on the larger balance. The borrower bought time with its own future. That is not fraud and not necessarily a mistake — a good business with a bad year is exactly what forbearance is for — but it is also how losses get deferred rather than recognised. A traded loan would reprice immediately: its market price would fall, and everyone would see the loss. A private loan is valued by the fund itself, typically quarterly, using models rather than market prices. The loss may show up slowly, or not at all until the loan is restructured. This is the "stale valuation" problem the IMF chapter emphasises: when valuations move slowly, investors can believe a fund is earning a smooth 10% right up until the moment it is not.

A related drift is worth naming. Early private credit loans typically carried maintenance covenants — financial tests, like a maximum leverage ratio, checked every quarter, giving the lender an early right to intervene. As competition among lenders intensified, many loans went "covenant-lite," with only incurrence tests that bite when the borrower tries to do something new, not when its performance deteriorates. The lender traded an early warning system for market share. The borrower, naturally, accepted.

## 6. Why Lenders Accept Illiquidity — and What Could Break

If private credit loans are illiquid, unrated, and exposed to exactly the borrowers banks avoid, why do sophisticated investors fund them? Three reasons, in descending order of legitimacy.

The first is the **illiquidity premium**: investors who genuinely do not need their money back for ten years — pension funds paying retirees, insurers matching long liabilities — can earn extra return for bearing illiquidity that would be intolerable for a mutual fund facing daily redemptions. That is a real economic service, and it is the core of the market's case for itself.

The second is **floating rates and seniority**. Unlike a long-dated bond, a private loan reprices with SOFR, so rising rates raise the lender's income rather than destroying the value of its holdings. And the loan is senior and secured: in a restructuring, the direct lender is typically first in line, ahead of equity and unsecured creditors. Recovery rates on first-lien loans have historically been far higher than on unsecured bonds, which is why lenders tolerate higher borrower leverage.

The third reason is less comfortable: **reported smoothness**. A fund that marks its loans quarterly, by model, reports far less volatility than a fund holding traded loans marked daily by the market — even if the underlying credit risk is identical. Investors who judge a strategy by its reported volatility will over-allocate to the smoother-looking one. That is not a criticism of any individual manager; it is a structural bias in how the product is measured, and it means the market's true risk is higher than its reported risk.

So what could actually break? The honest answer is that nobody knows, because the market has never lived through a severe downturn at its current size — the IMF's central caveat. Four candidate breakages are worth distinguishing:

1. **A delayed-loss problem.** If PIK amendments and model valuations defer recognition, a downturn could produce a sudden spike in defaults and markdowns after a long period of calm, rather than the gradual deterioration traded markets show. The shock would be concentrated in time, which is worse for investor confidence.
2. **A liquidity problem, but only in the semi-liquid corner.** Closed-end funds cannot be run on. Semi-liquid funds that offer quarterly redemptions against illiquid loans can be, if redemptions spike while loans cannot be sold. Watch that segment, not the whole market.
3. **An interconnectedness problem.** Banks lend to private credit funds, insurers and pensions invest in them, and private equity sponsors both own the borrowers and manage the lenders. A loss in one place can travel through several balance sheets before it stops.
4. **A plain old credit cycle.** The least exotic and most likely: leverage is high, borrowers are riskier than public-market peers, and a recession would raise defaults. Private credit would lose money the same way any lender does. The novelty is not that it can lose money; it is that the losses would be harder to see coming and slower to surface.

None of this means private credit is a bubble or a fraud. It means the market's pitch — high yield, low volatility, senior secured — bundles three claims that are partly in tension: the yield is real, the seniority is real, and the low volatility is partly an artefact of how the assets are valued. A clear-eyed investor holds all three thoughts at once.

## 7. Common Misconceptions

**"Private credit is shadow banking, so it is unregulated and dangerous."** Partly wrong on both counts. Much of the market is lightly regulated compared with banks, but BDCs are regulated investment companies with leverage limits, funds have auditors and valuation policies, and the largest managers are supervised entities. "Shadow" describes the migration of lending outside banks, not a legal vacuum. The risk is opacity, not lawlessness.

**"A unitranche loan is cheaper because there is only one rate."** As section 4 shows, it is usually more expensive than the blended rate of the tranches it replaces — by design. The borrower is buying simplicity, speed, and certainty, not a discount. Anyone who tells you the single rate is the whole story is hiding the OID, the fees, and the amendment economics.

**"Direct lenders do not lose money because they are senior and secured."** Seniority improves recovery; it does not prevent default. A first-lien lender to a company whose EBITDA halves can still lose principal, especially if PIK amendments have grown the loan balance in the meantime. Seniority is about how much you recover, not whether the borrower survives.

**"Private credit returns are uncorrelated with public markets."** Reported returns look smooth and uncorrelated largely because valuations are infrequent. The underlying borrowers sell into the same economy, borrow at rates set by the same central banks, and default in the same recessions. When the cycle turns, the correlation will show up — late, and all at once.

**"Banks are out of leveraged lending."** Banks still arrange most syndicated loans and lend to private credit funds themselves, as subscription-line and fund-level leverage providers. The risk did not leave the banking system; it changed form, from direct borrower exposure to exposure to the funds that now hold the loans.

## 8. Test Yourself

**1. MidCo's unitranche is $480 million at SOFR + 550 bps with SOFR at 4.30%. A rival lender offers a syndicated-style stack: $360 million first lien at SOFR + 425 bps and $120 million second lien at SOFR + 800 bps. Which is cheaper per year, and by how much?**

The stack: first-lien interest $360m × 8.55% = $30.78m; second-lien interest $120m × 12.30% = $14.76m; total $45.54m. The unitranche: $480m × 9.80% = $47.04m. The stack is cheaper by $47.04m − $45.54m = $1.50m per year. (Whether it is the better choice is a different question — see section 3.)

**2. A private loan of $200 million carries a 9.00% coupon, 2 points of OID, and a five-year maturity. Roughly what is the lender's all-in yield, ignoring fees and losses?**

OID of 2 points means the lender funds $196 million for $200 million of face, a $4 million discount. Spread over five years, that is about $0.8 million a year, or 0.40 percentage points on face. All-in yield ≈ 9.00% + 0.40% = 9.40%. (A precise internal-rate-of-return calculation would give a slightly different number, since the discount is received upfront, but 9.40% is the right order of magnitude.)

**3. A borrower with $500 million of debt and $70 million of EBITDA agrees a PIK toggle on 3.00 percentage points of its coupon for one year. How much debt does it have at year-end, and what is its new leverage ratio if EBITDA is unchanged?**

PIK interest = $500m × 3.00% = $15 million, added to principal. Year-end debt = $515 million. New leverage = $515m / $70m = 7.36x, up from 7.14x. Cash interest fell, but leverage rose — the trade in one line.

**4. Why might a private credit fund report lower volatility than a fund holding identical loans that trade publicly, and why should an investor care?**

Because the private fund values its loans quarterly using models, while the traded fund is marked daily by market prices. The private fund's reported volatility is smoothed by infrequent valuation, not by lower risk. An investor who allocates based on reported volatility will over-allocate to the private fund and be surprised when losses surface in a downturn — the stale-valuation problem of section 5.
