# 43 | Permanent Capital: The Fund That Never Has to Sell

*Articles 41 and 42 were both about the same problem wearing different clothes. In 41, a sponsor sold a good company to another sponsor because the fund's clock had run out. In 42, a sponsor sold a good company to itself for the same reason. Both articles treated the ten-year fund life as a fact of nature — a deadline to be managed, dodged, or worked around. This article asks the obvious next question: what if you simply refuse the deadline? What if the vehicle that owns the company never has to sell it, never has to return the capital, and never has to go fundraising again? That vehicle exists, in several different forms, and the biggest asset managers in the world are quietly rebuilding themselves around it. This is permanent capital — the fund that never has to sell.*

> **Learning goals.** After this article you can: (1) define permanent capital and explain how it differs from a closed-end fund, a continuation fund, and a fund extension; (2) explain why the ten-year fund life creates the problems that permanent capital solves — the forced sale, the fundraising treadmill, and the double carry; (3) name the four main forms permanent capital takes — listed vehicles, insurance balance sheets, evergreen funds, and holding companies — and say how each one actually works; (4) compute what a permanent fee stream is worth to a manager versus a ten-year fund, and show the arithmetic for an investor who holds a company for ten years instead of selling at year five and reinvesting; (5) explain the trade-off the academic literature identifies — the fund-life clock is a discipline device, and removing it creates a new agency problem; (6) judge a permanent capital vehicle from the investor's side, including fees, valuation, redemption terms, and alignment.
>
> **Prerequisites.** Article 08 (LBO) for sources and uses, MOIC, IRR, fees, and carry; article 39 (take-privates) for the buyout as a starting point; article 41 (secondary buyouts) for the fund-life clock and the exit problem; article 42 (continuation funds) for the most recent workaround — permanent capital is what you build when you stop patching the clock and remove it.

## 1. The Idea in One Paragraph

**Permanent capital** is capital that has no fixed date on which it must be returned. A traditional private equity fund is a **closed-end** vehicle: it raises money, invests it over about five years, and must sell everything and return the cash within about ten. Permanent capital has no such expiry. It comes in four main forms — a listed investment company whose shares trade on a stock exchange, an insurance balance sheet whose liabilities are annuity payments stretching decades into the future, an **evergreen fund** that is open-ended and lets investors subscribe and redeem periodically instead of committing for a decade, and a holding company that simply owns businesses outright and never sells them. The assets in these vehicles can be held for five years, fifteen years, or forever, because nobody's calendar forces a sale. That single difference — no expiry date — changes the economics for the manager, for the investor, and for the company being owned. Everything else in this article is working out the consequences.

The distinction to hold onto is between the *capital* and the *asset*. In a closed-end fund, the capital has a life (ten years) and the asset must be sold before the capital dies. In a permanent capital vehicle, the capital has no life, so the asset's holding period is decided on its merits: hold it while it compounds, sell it when the work is done or the price is right. The continuation fund in article 42 was a clever way to move an asset from expiring capital to fresh capital. Permanent capital skips the move entirely — the capital was never going to expire in the first place.

## 2. Why the Ten-Year Clock Is the Problem

The ten-year fund life was not an accident. It was designed, in the 1980s, as a discipline device: give a manager a fixed pot of money and a fixed deadline, and the deadline forces decisions. But three features of the modern market have turned that discipline into a cost.

**The forced sale.** Article 41 described the exit drought: Bain & Company estimated in 2025 that buyout funds hold roughly 29,000 unsold portfolio companies worth about $3.6 trillion. When the IPO window is shut and strategic buyers are cautious, a fund in year nine or ten must still sell — because the fund agreement says so, not because the company is ready. A forced sale into a thin market is how value gets destroyed quietly: not by a bad decision, but by a calendar.

**The fundraising treadmill.** A manager that raises a new fund every three to four years spends a large part of its senior partners' time fundraising rather than investing. The fund's life also creates a perverse incentive around the *next* fund: a GP that needs to show distributions to raise Fund III has a reason to sell Fund II's assets quickly, even at mediocre prices, because distributions are the currency of fundraising. The clock does not just force sales; it forces sales at the moment the manager most needs a headline.

**The double carry.** Every time a fund sells and the investor reinvests in a new fund, the manager's carry — typically 20% of the gain — is crystallised and paid again on the same underlying compounding. Section 5 puts numbers on this: on a good asset held for ten years, selling at year five and reinvesting costs the investor a second carry event, plus the time the money sits in cash waiting to be deployed. Permanent capital has one carry event, or none at all, because there is no sale to crystallise it.

None of this means the ten-year fund is a bad structure. Section 6 explains why it exists and what discipline it provides. But the costs are real, they grow as holding periods lengthen, and they explain why the industry's largest managers have spent the last decade building vehicles that do not expire.

## 3. The Four Flavours of Permanent Capital

Permanent capital is not one product. It is a property — no expiry date — that shows up in four quite different structures. A banker needs to know all four, because they behave differently, they are regulated differently, and they attract different investors.

**Flavour 1: the listed vehicle.** The oldest form. A listed investment company raises money once, by selling shares on a stock exchange, and the shares then trade freely — the investor who wants out sells to another investor, not back to the fund, so the fund itself never has to sell an asset to meet a redemption. 3i Group in London is the classic example: a listed private equity investor that has owned companies for decades without a fund-life deadline. There is a second, subtler listed form: the asset manager *itself* going public. When KKR, Apollo, and Blackstone listed their own shares, the management company became a permanent capital vehicle for its owners — the listed manager never has to return its own equity. Note the difference: a listed *fund* gives the investor permanent exposure to the assets; a listed *manager* gives the investor permanent exposure to the fee stream. Both are permanent capital, and they are not the same thing.

**Flavour 2: the insurance balance sheet.** This is the form that has reshaped the industry in the last five years, and it deserves the most attention. An insurer collects premiums today and pays claims years or decades later. In between, it holds the money — the **float** — and invests it. The float is permanent capital in the most literal sense: it does not have to be returned on a schedule, it grows as the insurer writes new policies, and it can be invested in illiquid assets because the liabilities are long-dated. Apollo understood this first. It helped found Athene, a retirement-services and annuity company, in 2009, managed Athene's assets for a fee, and then merged with Athene outright in January 2022 — a deal worth roughly $11 billion that brought Athene's balance sheet inside Apollo permanently. By the end of 2021, Apollo's permanent capital vehicles already held $296 billion of assets, about 59% of its total assets under management, with Athene alone at roughly $213 billion. KKR followed the same playbook: it announced the acquisition of Global Atlantic Financial Group in July 2020, closed the deal in February 2021 for about $4.4 billion, and took its permanent capital from 9% of assets under management to 33% in one transaction — its total AUM rising from $207 billion to $279 billion on a pro forma basis. Global Atlantic brought more than $70 billion of invested assets and over two million policyholders. The pattern is now the industry's central strategic move: own the capital, don't just manage it.

**Flavour 3: the evergreen fund.** The newest and fastest-growing form, and the one aimed at individual investors rather than institutions. An evergreen fund is open-ended: there is no fixed fundraising period and no termination date, and investors can subscribe and redeem periodically — typically quarterly — instead of committing capital for ten years. The family includes business development companies (BDCs), interval funds, tender-offer funds, and non-traded REITs. The scale is now material: PitchBook estimated total evergreen fund assets at roughly $2.7 trillion at the end of 2024, across all strategies, and projected growth to about $4.4 trillion by 2029. The wealth-focused slice alone stood at $427 billion in the US and Europe in 2024, forecast to grow at roughly 20% a year to about $1.1 trillion by 2029. The catch — and it is a large one — is that the liquidity is only semi-real. The fund offers quarterly redemptions, but the assets are illiquid, so the redemption right is capped and gated. Section 8 comes back to this.

**Flavour 4: the holding company.** The purest form, and the oldest. A holding company buys businesses and simply never sells them. Berkshire Hathaway is the archetype: an insurance float (from GEICO and National Indemnity) funding outright ownership of operating businesses, with no fund, no fees, no carry, and no exit deadline. The holding company is permanent capital without any of the fund machinery at all — which is precisely why it is so hard to replicate. It requires a manager willing to be judged over decades, and investors willing to let him.

| Form | Who provides the capital | How the investor exits | The catch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Listed vehicle | Public shareholders | Sell the shares on the exchange | Shares can trade at a discount to NAV |
| Insurance balance sheet | Policyholders / annuitants | Policies mature or are surrendered | Regulated; the float must be invested conservatively |
| Evergreen fund | Individuals + institutions | Quarterly redemption windows | Redemptions are capped and gated |
| Holding company | Shareholders, indefinitely | Sell the shares | No fees, but also no diversification for the investor |

## 4. The Worked Example: What Permanent Capital Is Worth to the Manager

Start with the manager's side, because it explains the urgency. A fee stream that never ends is worth far more than a fee stream that stops in ten years — and the arithmetic is the reason every large manager is racing to build permanent capital.

**Fund X — the traditional closed-end fund.** A $1,000 million fund charges a 2% annual management fee on committed capital and lives for ten years. The fee is $20 million a year, every year, for ten years — $200 million in total. But fees received in year ten are worth less than fees received today, so discount them at 10% a year. The present value of a ten-year $20 million annuity at 10% is:

```math
PV = $20m × [(1 − 1.10^(−10)) / 0.10] = $20m × 6.1446 = $122.9m
```

**Vehicle Y — the permanent capital vehicle.** The same $1,000 million of assets, now in a permanent vehicle that charges a lower fee — 1.25% — because permanent capital is cheaper for the investor. The first year's fee is $12.5 million. But the assets grow: suppose the vehicle grows at 6% a year net of distributions, so the fee grows at 6% a year too, forever. The present value of a growing perpetuity is the first payment divided by the discount rate minus the growth rate:

```math
PV = $12.5m / (0.10 − 0.06) = $12.5m / 0.04 = $312.5m
```

The same $1 billion of capital is worth **$312.5 million** to the manager as permanent capital, against **$122.9 million** as a ten-year fund — about two and a half times as much — even though the fee *rate* is lower. Two details make the comparison honest. First, the permanent vehicle's fee in year ten is $12.5m × 1.06^9 ≈ $21.1 million — still being paid, and still growing, in the year Fund X pays its last dollar. Second, the trade is not free for the manager: over the first ten years the permanent vehicle collects only about $165 million in fees, less than Fund X's $200 million, because the rate is lower. The manager gives up near-term fees for an infinite tail. That is a trade any manager with a long horizon is delighted to make — and it is why the public markets value asset managers with large permanent capital bases at higher multiples: the fee stream is closer to a subscription than to a series of one-off fundraisings.

## 5. The Worked Example, Continued: What It Costs — and Saves — the Investor

Now the investor's side, and here the answer is more interesting than the sales pitch. Permanent capital does not magically create return. What it removes is friction: the forced sale, the second carry, and the redeployment lag. **Harbor Logistics** is a fictional freight company. All numbers are in millions of dollars, and two assumptions are held fixed throughout so the comparison is clean: the entry and exit multiple stays at 10.0x EBITDA, and EBITDA grows at a smooth 12% a year.

**The buy (Year 0).** EBITDA is $50 million. Harbor is bought at 10.0x — an enterprise value of $500 million — with debt of 5.0x EBITDA and the rest equity.

```math
Entry EV = 10.0 × $50m = $500m
Debt at entry = 5.0 × $50m = $250m
Sponsor equity = $500m − $250m = $250m
```

**Five years later (Year 5).** EBITDA has grown at 12% a year to $50m × 1.12^5 = $88.1 million. At the same 10.0x multiple, the enterprise value is $881 million. Debt has been paid down by $10 million a year to $200 million.

```math
Exit EV at Year 5 = 10.0 × $88.1m = $881m
Equity value = $881m − $200m = $681m
MOIC = $681m / $250m = 2.72x
IRR = 2.72^(1/5) − 1 ≈ 22.2%
```

**Path A: the fund must sell.** A closed-end fund in year five of its life is already thinking about its exit. It sells Harbor, crystallising carry: 20% of the $431 million gain, or about $86 million. The LP nets roughly $595 million and must reinvest it — in a new fund, at a new entry multiple, after the new fund finishes deploying.

**Path B: permanent capital holds.** The vehicle simply keeps Harbor. Five more years of 12% growth take EBITDA to $50m × 1.12^10 = $155.3 million, an enterprise value of $1,553 million, and debt paid down to $150 million.

```math
Equity value at Year 10 = $1,553m − $150m = $1,403m
MOIC = $1,403m / $250m = 5.61x
IRR = 5.61^(1/10) − 1 ≈ 18.8%
```

**Path A, completed: sell and reinvest.** Suppose the LP reinvests the $595 million instantly, into an identical deal — same entry multiple, same growth, same five-year horizon. The reinvested money earns the same 2.72x MOIC, growing to about $1,621 million gross. But carry is crystallised a second time: 20% of the $1,026 million gain, or about $205 million. The LP nets about **$1,416 million** — against **$1,403 million** from simply holding. With instant, frictionless redeployment, the forced sale costs almost nothing: the second carry is offset by the fact that the new deal re-levers the capital, restoring the debt that the old deal had paid down.

That is the honest result, and it is worth pausing on, because it cuts against the sales pitch. Permanent capital does not win because holding is magically better than selling. It wins when redeployment is *not* instant and *not* frictionless — which is the normal case. Add a single year of deployment lag — the money sits in cash for a year while the new fund finds its deals, so the reinvested capital gets only four years of growth instead of five — and the reinvestment path falls to about $1,217 million net of the second carry. The gap between holding and selling is now roughly **$186 million**, on a $250 million starting investment. Add a second year of lag, or a worse entry multiple in the new deal, and the gap widens further. The permanent capital advantage is not compounding. It is the removal of three specific frictions — the second carry event, the cash drag of redeployment, and the risk that the next deal is worse than the one you were forced to sell — and on a good asset those frictions are worth a fortune.

| | Hold 10 years (permanent) | Sell at Year 5, reinvest instantly | Sell at Year 5, 1-year deployment lag |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Equity value at Year 10 (gross) | $1,403m | $1,621m | $1,373m |
| Carry paid | $0 | $86m + $205m = $291m | $86m + $156m = $242m |
| LP net at Year 10 | **$1,403m** | **$1,416m** | **$1,217m** |
| Second carry event? | No | Yes | Yes |

One more honest note: the permanent vehicle charges fees too, and this comparison holds fees aside on both sides. In practice the permanent vehicle usually charges a *lower* headline fee than the closed-end fund (1–1.5% rather than 2%), which widens the gap in its favour — but the carry is the bigger item, and the carry is where the real money is.

## 6. What the Evidence Says

Academic research does not use the phrase "permanent capital" very often, but the two papers that explain the closed-end fund's structure also explain, by implication, what you lose when you remove it.

**Why the fund looks the way it does.** Kaplan & Strömberg (2009), *"Leveraged Buyouts and Private Equity"*, Journal of Economic Perspectives 23(1): 121–146, is the standard description of the industry's structure: the ten-year closed-end fund, the 2% management fee, the 20% carry, and the holding periods that follow. One number from the paper is worth remembering: only 12% of buyout deals are exited within 24 months of acquisition. Deals take years to work, and the fund's life is the mechanism that forces the manager to eventually realise value rather than hold assets indefinitely and keep collecting fees. That is the discipline the clock provides — and it is a real one.

**The clock as a discipline device.** Axelson, Strömberg & Weisbach (2009), *"Why Are Buyouts Levered? The Financial Structure of Private Equity Funds"*, Journal of Finance 64(4): 1549–1582, model the fund's structure as a solution to an agency problem: the manager has an incentive to overstate the quality of investments, and the fund structure — capital raised up front, carry paid on the *aggregate* performance of the whole fund, and deal-by-deal debt that disappears when discipline fails — is what keeps that incentive in check. Read that result in reverse and you get the central trade-off of this article. The ten-year life, the fixed capital, and the eventual forced sale are not design flaws. They are the mechanisms that stop a manager from raising money, doing nothing, and collecting fees forever. Permanent capital removes the deadline — and with it, one of the main devices that keeps the manager honest. Whatever replaces the deadline has to do the same job: governance, valuation discipline, redemption rights, or the manager's own capital at risk. A permanent vehicle without a substitute discipline is just a fee machine.

**The market's vote.** The volume figures in section 3 are the strongest evidence available, and they point in one direction: PitchBook's estimate of $2.7 trillion in evergreen assets at the end of 2024, growing toward $4.4 trillion by 2029, and the fact that the two largest alternative managers rebuilt themselves around insurance balance sheets within three years of each other. But the same market has also supplied the warning. The Nadauld, Sensoy, Vorkink & Weisbach (2019) result from article 42 — that buyers of secondary liquidity outperform sellers by about five percentage points a year — is a reminder that liquidity has a price, and in a permanent vehicle the price is paid in gates and discounts rather than in a sale. And the BREIT episode in section 8 shows what happens when the liquidity promise meets a stress case.

## 7. The Famous Example: Apollo and Athene

No deal illustrates permanent capital better than Apollo's merger with Athene, because Apollo did not buy a company. It bought a *liability structure* — and then rebuilt itself around it.

Athene began in 2009 as a retirement-services company that Apollo helped create. The idea was simple: Athene would sell fixed annuities to retirees, collect the premiums, and Apollo would manage the resulting assets, investing them in the credit strategies it knew best and earning the spread between the yield on the assets and the rate promised to annuitants. The annuity liabilities were long-dated and sticky — policyholders do not all surrender at once — so the float behaved like permanent capital. For twelve years the arrangement ran as a partnership: Athene was a separate listed company, Apollo its investment manager.

In January 2022 the two merged outright, in an all-stock deal worth roughly $11 billion. Apollo's reasoning was the arithmetic of section 4, applied to its own business: the fee stream on Athene's assets was valuable, but owning the balance sheet was worth more. The merged Apollo kept both businesses under one roof — asset management and retirement services — and the combined company's earnings became a mix of management fees and the investment spread on a permanent insurance portfolio. By the end of 2021, before the merger even closed, Apollo's permanent capital vehicles already held $296 billion, about 59% of its total assets under management, with Athene at roughly $213 billion of that.

KKR's version of the same move came a year earlier. It announced the acquisition of Global Atlantic in July 2020 and closed it in February 2021 for about $4.4 billion, taking its permanent capital from 9% to 33% of assets under management in a single transaction. The two deals together are the industry's clearest statement of intent: the largest managers no longer want to rent their capital from pension funds for ten years at a time. They want to own it.

Apollo's deal is also a useful reminder of what permanent capital does *not* do. It does not make the underlying investing better. Athene's float is only as good as the credit Apollo puts it into, and the spread is only earned if the assets perform and the liabilities are priced correctly. The structure buys time and removes the fundraising treadmill. It does not buy skill.

## 8. Judging a Permanent Capital Vehicle: A Checklist

Permanent capital shifts the investor's question. In a closed-end fund you ask: is this manager good at buying, fixing, and selling companies within ten years? In a permanent vehicle you ask: is this manager good at *owning* things indefinitely, and what protects me if they stop being good? Five questions, in order.

**1. What is the fee, and what is it charged on?** Compare like with like. A permanent vehicle charging 1.25% on assets every year forever can cost more over twenty years than a closed-end fund charging 2% for ten years — section 4 shows why the manager loves the trade, and the investor should do the same present-value math from the other side. Ask whether there is also a performance fee or carry, and on what it is charged: on realised gains only, or on annual NAV appreciation that may never be realised?

**2. Who sets the value, and how often?** A closed-end fund is valued when it sells. A permanent vehicle is valued continuously — quarterly NAV strikes, monthly estimates — and the manager usually has a hand in the valuation. Evergreen funds have made valuation integrity a live issue: the NAV is the price at which new investors buy in and redeeming investors cash out, so a stale or generous NAV transfers value between investors. Ask who values the assets, whether an independent administrator or auditor is involved, and how often the valuation is refreshed. If the answer is vague, the vehicle is a black box with a quarterly price tag.

**3. What are the redemption terms, really?** Read the gate before you invest, not after you want out. The standard evergreen structure caps redemptions at 2% of NAV per month and 5% per quarter, and the cap applies to the whole fund, not to you individually — when requests exceed the cap, everyone is prorated. Blackstone's BREIT is the case study: in November 2022, after redemption requests blew through both caps, the fund met only 43% of the month's requests, and it took more than a year to clear the backlog. In January 2023, investors asked to withdraw $5.3 billion and received about $1.3 billion. The gate worked exactly as written — which is the point. The liquidity in an evergreen fund is a contractual promise with a ceiling, and the ceiling binds precisely when you most want it not to.

**4. How much of the manager's own money is in the vehicle?** In a closed-end fund the GP commits 1–5% of the fund. In a permanent vehicle the alignment question is sharper, because there is no fund end to force a reckoning. The encouraging sign is co-investment at scale: when the University of California put $4 billion into BREIT in January 2023, Blackstone backstopped the arrangement with $1 billion of its own BREIT shares. The discouraging sign is a manager that charges fees on a vehicle it would not own itself.

**5. What is the governance — who can fire the manager?** A closed-end fund's investors can decline to commit to the next fund, which is the ultimate sanction. A permanent vehicle's investors cannot. So ask: can investors remove the manager, and by what vote? Is there an independent board? Does the manager control the vehicle, the valuation, and the redemption policy at the same time? Permanent capital concentrates power in the manager. The governance has to be strong enough to carry that weight, and in most of these vehicles it is the weakest part of the structure.

## 9. Common Misconceptions

**"Permanent capital means the investor can never get their money back."** No — it means the *vehicle* never has to return it. The investor's exit depends on the form: sell listed shares on the exchange, surrender an annuity, or redeem from the evergreen fund under its quarterly terms. The confusion comes from conflating the vehicle's life with the investor's liquidity. A listed vehicle is permanent capital with daily liquidity; an evergreen fund is permanent capital with gated quarterly liquidity; a closed-end fund is temporary capital with no liquidity at all until it sells. Permanence and liquidity are separate questions.

**"Permanent capital is always better for the investor."** Not always, and section 5 shows why the advantage is narrower than the pitch suggests. On a good asset with instant redeployment, selling and reinvesting is roughly a wash. The advantage appears only when redeployment is slow, the next deal is worse, or the market is bad at the forced-sale date. And the investor pays for permanence in other ways: lower headline fees that run forever, NAV-based valuations they cannot verify, and redemption gates that bind in a crisis. Permanence is a trade, not a free lunch.

**"Evergreen funds are liquid."** They are semi-liquid, and the "semi" does the work. Quarterly redemption windows, 2% monthly and 5% quarterly caps, proration across all redeeming investors, and the manager's right to suspend redemptions entirely in stressed conditions. BREIT's fourteen months of prorated redemptions from late 2022 are the honest advertisement for the product: the liquidity is real in normal times and rationed in bad times — which is exactly when investors want it most.

**"Insurance float is free money."** The float is cheap, not free. The insurer promises annuitants a return, bears the risk that they live longer than expected, and is regulated on how it invests. The spread between the asset yield and the liability cost is the profit, and it can go negative if the assets underperform or the liabilities are mispriced. Apollo's Athene works because the liabilities are long-dated and the credit investing is disciplined. An insurer that reaches for yield to fund an expensive liability book is a disaster with a time delay.

**"Removing the ten-year clock removes the discipline."** Only if nothing replaces it. The clock was one discipline device among several — carry on aggregate fund performance, deal-by-deal debt, the need to raise the next fund. A permanent vehicle needs substitutes: an independent board, transparent valuation, meaningful co-investment, and redemption rights that let unhappy investors leave. The vehicles that have these are genuinely better structures. The vehicles that do not are, as Axelson, Strömberg & Weisbach would put it, a fee stream in search of an agency problem.

**"Berkshire Hathaway proves anyone can do this."** Berkshire proves it can be done, once, by a manager with a fifty-year record, a fortress balance sheet, and shareholders who treat the stock as a permanent holding. It does not prove the structure is easy. Most permanent capital vehicles are run by managers who still have to earn their investors' trust one quarter at a time — and the structure gives them more rope, in both directions, than a ten-year fund ever did.

That is about $13m *more* than the $1,403m from holding — a wash. With instant, frictionless redeployment, the forced sale costs almost nothing, because the new deal re-levers the capital.

**3.** With one year of cash drag, the reinvested capital gets four years of growth: EBITDA = $50m × 1.12^4 = **$78.7m**; EV = **$787m**; debt = $250m − $40m = $210m; equity = **$577m**; MOIC = 577/250 = **2.31x**. The LP's $595m grows to $595m × 2.307 ≈ **$1,373m** gross; gain = $1,373m − $595m = $778m; second carry = 20% × $778m ≈ **$156m**; LP nets ≈ **$1,217m**. That is about **$186m less** than the $1,403m from holding. The lesson: permanent capital's advantage is not compounding — it is the removal of friction. One year of deployment lag plus a second carry event is worth roughly $186m on a $250m starting investment. Add a worse entry multiple or a second year of lag and the gap grows.

**4.** Four concerns: **(a)** the carry is charged on *annual NAV appreciation* — the manager gets paid on paper gains that may never be realised, with no fund end to force a true-up; want carry on realised gains only, or a clawback. **(b)** the portfolio is valued internally once a year — the NAV is both the subscription price and the redemption price, and the manager sets it; want independent valuation, more frequent, with an external administrator or auditor. **(c)** the redemption caps (2% monthly, 5% quarterly) mean the investor cannot exit in size when it matters — BREIT met only 43% of November 2022 requests; want to understand the gate, the proration, and whether redemptions can be suspended. **(d)** there is no fund-life deadline and no next fundraise to discipline the manager — the Axelson, Strömberg & Weisbach point — so the vehicle needs substitute discipline: an independent board, meaningful GP co-investment, and a real mechanism to remove the manager. Without those, it is a fee stream with no expiry date and no accountability.
