# 40 | Dividend Recapitalizations: When an LBO Pays Its Owners Back

*In article 08, the LBO made sense on one condition: the sponsor only gets paid at the exit. It buys the company, fixes it, sells it five years later, and the return is whatever is left after the debt is repaid. That is the textbook version. But in real life, sponsors have found a way to get paid in the middle — without selling anything. They borrow more money against the company they already own, and pay the proceeds out to themselves as a dividend. The company keeps operating, the sponsor keeps owning it, and yet a large chunk of the sponsor's cash comes home years early. This article is about that transaction: the dividend recapitalization. How it works, why it makes the return numbers look better, what it costs the company, and when it crosses the line from smart financial engineering into recklessness.*

> **Learning goals.** After this article you can: (1) define a dividend recapitalization ("dividend recap") and explain how it differs from a refinancing and from an exit; (2) walk through the mechanics — new debt, restricted-payment covenants, and the pro-rata dividend; (3) compute how a recap changes MOIC and IRR, and explain why the two move in opposite directions; (4) explain the four reasons sponsors do recaps, including the fund-level metrics (DPI) that drive the decision; (5) describe what the research finds about the consequences for firms, employees, and creditors; (6) judge whether a given recap is prudent or reckless.
>
> **Prerequisites.** Article 03 (enterprise value vs equity value) for the bridge; article 08 (LBO) for sources and uses, MOIC, IRR, and leverage; article 25 (leveraged finance) for how the debt is raised and what covenants constrain; article 39 (take-privates) for HCA, the most famous recap story in the market.

## 1. The Idea in One Paragraph

A **dividend recapitalization** — a **dividend recap**, or just a **recap** — is a transaction in which a private equity-owned company takes on new debt and immediately pays the borrowed money out to its shareholders as a dividend. Nothing is sold. Ownership does not change. The sponsor still owns the same percentage of the same company the day after the recap as the day before. What changes is the capital structure: the company has more debt, less equity cushion, and a larger interest bill — and the sponsor has a large sum of cash back in its fund, years before any exit.

That is the whole transaction, and it is worth pausing on how strange it is. In a normal dividend, a company pays its owners out of profits it has earned. In a recap, the company pays its owners out of money it has borrowed. The cash does not come from the business; it comes from lenders betting the business will generate enough cash in the future to repay a bigger debt stack. The sponsor is, in effect, pulling future cash flow forward into the present and pocketing it. Whether that is brilliant or dangerous depends entirely on whether the future cash flow actually arrives.

## 2. Why Would a Sponsor Do This? Four Reasons

Sponsors do not do recaps because they are bored. Each reason is a real incentive, and the fourth is the one that explains the timing — why recaps cluster in certain years and vanish in others.

**1. Getting cash back early — and the DPI that comes with it.** A private equity fund is judged by its limited partners (LPs) on two numbers: IRR and **DPI**, distributions to paid-in capital — the cash the fund has actually returned divided by the cash the LPs put in. DPI is the number LPs can spend. An exit returns cash, but exits take five to seven years and depend on a buyer showing up. A recap returns cash on the sponsor's timetable, without needing a buyer at all. And a fund that is distributing cash is a fund that can raise its next fund: LPs commit to new funds when the old fund is showing them money, not paper gains.

**2. The IRR effect — the time value of the sponsor's own money.** This is the mathematical heart of the recap, and section 4 works it out in full. Money returned in year 3 is worth more than the same money returned in year 5, because IRR rewards speed. A recap can lift a deal's IRR by several percentage points even when it does not change the total dollars the sponsor eventually receives — and sometimes even when it slightly reduces them. This is not a trick; it is the time value of money applied to the sponsor's own capital. But it is also the reason IRR can mislead: a higher IRR after a recap does not mean the deal got better. It means the sponsor got paid sooner, and the company's higher risk does not appear in the IRR.

**3. De-risking the position.** Before a recap, the sponsor has its entire equity cheque at risk in the company. After a recap that returns 75% of that cheque, only 25% is still at risk — and the sponsor still owns the whole company. If the business then stumbles, the sponsor has already banked most of its money. This is the same logic as selling part of a position in the public markets, except there is no buyer and no sale: the sponsor de-risks by borrowing against the asset rather than selling it. HCA is the canonical example — section 6 tells that story.

**4. Cheap credit windows.** Recaps happen when debt is cheap and plentiful, and they stop when it is not. The sponsor is not doing the recap because the company suddenly needs money; it is doing it because the credit market is offering money on attractive terms right now. That opportunism is rational — borrow when borrowing is cheap — but it means the timing of a recap tells you about the credit market, not about the company. A recap announced in a hot leveraged-loan market is a statement about the loan market. The research in section 5 confirms this: sponsors do recaps precisely when their relationship banks can offer them cheaper credit than their peers get.

There is a fifth reason, quieter and worth naming: **management gets paid too.** The dividend is paid pro rata to all equity holders, including management's rollover equity from the original buyout. A recap puts cash in the management team's pockets years before an exit, which is a powerful retention tool — and a reason management rarely objects to the extra leverage. Everyone at the table gets paid. The only parties not at the table are the company's future self, which has to service the new debt, and the existing lenders, whose claims just got pushed further down the queue in economic terms.

## 3. How a Recap Actually Works

A recap is a financing transaction plus a board resolution, and the financing is the hard part. The steps, in order:

**Check the covenants first.** The company's existing debt agreements control whether a recap is even possible. Two provisions matter. The **restricted payments covenant** limits the dividends a borrower can pay — typically to a "basket" built up from retained earnings and equity contributions, plus a general allowance. If the basket is too small, the recap cannot happen until the company has earned its way into a bigger one. The **leverage incurrence test** limits new debt: the company can only borrow more if, after the borrowing, its leverage stays below a specified multiple. These covenants are the lenders' protection, and they are the first thing a banker checks. A recap that requires a covenant amendment is a different, harder conversation — the lenders have to be persuaded, and they will charge for it.

**Raise the new debt.** The new money usually comes as an **incremental term loan** under the existing credit agreement, or as a new tranche of high-yield bonds. The pricing depends on the credit market at that moment, which is why recaps cluster in good markets. The new lenders do their own diligence, and they will ask the obvious question — why does this company need more debt when nothing about its operations has changed? The honest answer is that it does not need the debt; its owners want the cash. Lenders fund recaps anyway, because the company's cash flow supports the larger debt stack, and because fees are fees. But note the asymmetry: the new lender gets paid for taking the risk, and the old lender gets nothing except a more leveraged borrower.

**The board declares the dividend.** Once the cash is in the company's bank account, the board declares a dividend to shareholders. In a PE-owned company this is a formality — the sponsor controls the board — but it is still a legal act with a legal test: the dividend must not render the company insolvent, and directors must be satisfied the company can pay its debts as they come due.

**Cash goes out pro rata; ownership is unchanged.** Every equity holder receives the same dividend per share. The sponsor receives the large majority; management and any co-investors receive their share. Nobody sells anything, nobody's percentage changes, and the company keeps operating exactly as before — with one difference: it now owes more money, and more of its cash flow is spoken for by interest payments.

Three things a recap is **not**, because the confusion is common:

- **It is not a refinancing.** A refinancing replaces old debt with new debt, usually to get a lower rate or a longer maturity. The total debt stays roughly the same. A recap *adds* debt. The debt stack grows; that is the point.
- **It is not an exit.** An exit transfers ownership and ends the sponsor's involvement. A recap does the opposite: the sponsor stays, fully invested, and keeps the upside — while having already recovered much of its capital.
- **It is not free money.** The dividend is real cash in the sponsor's pocket, but it is borrowed cash, and the company will repay it out of future operating cash flow — cash that would otherwise have funded growth, deleveraging, or the exit proceeds. The sponsor has not created value; it has moved value forward in time and added risk to the company in exchange.

The Modigliani–Miller intuition from finance theory is worth remembering here: in a frictionless world, changing the capital structure does not change the value of the firm. The value created by a recap, if any, comes from the frictions — the tax deductibility of the extra interest, and the time value of returning cash to LPs sooner. The value destroyed comes from the other friction: the higher probability of financial distress, which section 5 quantifies.

## 4. The Worked Example: Harbor Logistics

Let us build the whole thing with numbers, because the recap's effect on returns is genuinely counterintuitive: it raises IRR, leaves MOIC roughly unchanged, and can lower MOIC once you count the interest — all at the same time. **Harbor Logistics** is a fictional trucking and warehousing company. All numbers are in millions of dollars.

**The entry (Year 0).** A sponsor buys Harbor Logistics for an enterprise value of $500 million. EBITDA is $50 million.

```math
Entry EV = $500m
Sponsor equity = $200m
Debt at entry = $300m  (6.0x EBITDA)
```

**Three years later (Year 3).** The plan is working. EBITDA has grown to $65 million, and the company has paid down $20 million of debt a year, so debt is now $240 million. The business is worth about 8.0x EBITDA, or $520 million, on paper — but the sponsor has no intention of selling yet.

```math
Year 3 EBITDA = $65m
Debt after paydown = $300m − 3 × $20m = $240m
Leverage now = $240m / $65m = 3.7x
```

Leverage has fallen from 6.0x to 3.7x, because the earnings grew and the debt shrank. That is the opening: the company has room to borrow again.

**The recap.** Harbor borrows $150 million of new debt and pays it straight out as a dividend. Total debt returns to $390 million — exactly 6.0x the new, higher EBITDA.

```math
New debt raised = $150m
Total debt after recap = $240m + $150m = $390m
Leverage after recap = $390m / $65m = 6.0x
Dividend to sponsor = $150m  (assuming the sponsor owns ~100% of the equity)
Sponsor capital returned = $150m / $200m = 75%
Capital still at risk = $200m − $150m = $50m
```

The sponsor has recovered three-quarters of its money, three years into a five-year hold, and still owns the entire company. Its remaining exposure is $50 million. That is the de-risking in section 2, in numbers.

The new debt is not free. At an 8% interest rate, the extra $150 million costs $12 million a year, and total interest on the $390 million stack is about $31 million a year against $65 million of EBITDA — interest coverage of about 2.1x. Comfortable, but no longer generous. This is the cost the company pays for the sponsor's early payday, and the line to watch if the business turns down.

**The exit (Year 5) — with and without the recap.** Assume EBITDA reaches $75 million by year 5, and the business sells at 8.0x EBITDA, for an enterprise value of $600 million. Assume the company pays down $20 million of debt a year in both cases.

*Without a recap:* debt at exit is $240m − 2 × $20m = $200m. Exit equity is $600m − $200m = $400m.

```math
MOIC (no recap) = $400m / $200m = 2.00x
IRR (no recap) = 2.00^(1/5) − 1 ≈ 14.9%
```

*With a recap, ignoring the extra interest:* debt at exit is $390m − 2 × $20m = $350m. Exit equity is $600m − $350m = $250m. The sponsor's cash flows are: −$200m at year 0, +$150m at year 3, +$250m at year 5.

```math
Total proceeds = $150m + $250m = $400m
MOIC (with recap) = $400m / $200m = 2.00x  — unchanged
IRR: −200 + 150/(1+r)^3 + 250/(1+r)^5 = 0  →  r ≈ 18%
```

Check the IRR: at 18%, 150/1.18³ = $91.3m and 250/1.18⁵ = $109.3m, totalling $200.6m — just above the $200m invested, so the IRR is a touch above 18%. The same $400 million of total proceeds, the same 2.00x MOIC — but the IRR is three percentage points higher, because $150 million of it arrived two years early. That is the whole trick: money sooner is worth more. It is also why a fund can report a better IRR after a recap without the deal having improved by a single dollar.

*With a recap, counting the extra interest:* the picture is less flattering, and more honest. The extra $150 million of debt costs $12 million a year in interest, which means $12 million a year less cash available for debt paydown in years 4 and 5 — $24 million in total. Debt at exit is therefore $374 million, not $350 million, and exit equity is $600m − $374m = $226m.

```math
Total proceeds (with interest drag) = $150m + $226m = $376m
MOIC = $376m / $200m = 1.88x  — lower than the no-recap 2.00x
IRR ≈ 16.5%  — still above the no-recap 14.9%, but below the 18% headline
```

Check the IRR: at 16.5%, 150/1.165³ = $94.9m and 226/1.165⁵ = $105.3m, totalling $200.2m — essentially the $200m invested. So the recap's real effect, once the interest is counted, is: IRR up by about 1.6 points, MOIC down from 2.00x to 1.88x. The sponsor traded total return for speed. Whether that trade is worth it depends on the fund's needs — and on the sponsor being honest with its LPs about which number moved and why.

| Scenario | Total proceeds | MOIC | IRR |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| No recap | $400m | 2.00x | ~14.9% |
| Recap, ignoring extra interest | $400m | 2.00x | ~18% |
| Recap, counting extra interest | $376m | 1.88x | ~16.5% |

**The stress test.** Now suppose EBITDA falls to $50 million in year 4 instead of growing to $75 million — a recession, a lost contract, a cost shock. Without the recap, leverage is $220m/$50m = 4.4x and interest coverage is comfortable. With the recap, leverage is $390m/$50m = 7.8x, and the $31 million interest bill eats more than 60% of EBITDA. If the debt agreement has a maximum leverage covenant of 6.5x, the company is in breach — and a breach means the lenders can demand repayment, block further borrowing, and force a restructuring. The sponsor has its $150 million safely in the fund. The company has the problem. That asymmetry is the moral of the recap, and it is what the research in the next section measures.

## 5. What the Research Says

Until recently, there was almost no rigorous research on dividend recaps — a market practice everyone knew about and nobody had studied causally. That changed with a working paper that is now the reference on the subject.

**Bhardwaj, Gupta, and Howell (2024)**, *"Leveraged Payouts: How Using New Debt to Pay Returns in Private Equity Affects Firms, Employees, Creditors, and Investors,"* NBER Working Paper No. 32948 (revised as Working Paper No. 33435, *"Capital Structure & Firm Outcomes: Evidence from Dividend Recapitalizations in Private Equity,"* January 2025). The authors study U.S. dividend recaps from PitchBook, LCD, and Dealscan data — 1,572 recaps out of more than 60,000 leveraged buyouts — and they solve the selection problem: the companies that get recaps are the big, high-quality ones, so a naive comparison would flatter the recap. They isolate the causal effect by exploiting the fact that sponsors do recaps when *their* relationship banks can offer cheaper credit than peers get. Their findings:

- **The leverage jump is large.** Total debt rises by about 84% on average in a recap. This is not a marginal top-up; it is a step-change in the capital structure.
- **Distress risk jumps with it.** The probability of financial distress rises to roughly 2.4 times the targeted firm's mean — in line with what the Altman Z-score model would predict for a leverage increase of this size. Bankruptcy and exit rates rise sharply.
- **Deal returns rise; fund returns fall.** The recap increases the return on the individual deal — the IRR effect from section 4 — but *reduces* the fund's overall returns, a pattern the authors attribute to moral hazard: a fund that can manufacture early distributions has an easier time raising its next fund, which weakens the discipline that would otherwise keep it from over-levering its companies.
- **Employees and existing creditors lose.** Wages fall among surviving firms, and the prices of the pre-existing loans fall — the old lenders' claims are worth less the moment the new debt arrives. The recap transfers value from the company's stakeholders to the sponsor's investors, and the transfer is measurable.
- **But not everything gets worse.** Recapped firms also show more revenue growth and more IPOs — consistent with the selection story (these are good companies) and with the possibility that returning capital to the fund lets it redeploy into new investments.

Read alongside **Kaplan and Strömberg (2009)**, *"Leveraged Buyouts and Private Equity,"* Journal of Economic Perspectives 23(1): 121–146 — the standard survey of what private equity does to companies — the picture is coherent. The LBO itself, done well, can improve operations and governance; the recap is a different transaction layered on top, and its effects are mostly about the capital structure rather than the operations. The operations may keep improving. The balance sheet gets more dangerous. Both things can be true at once.

One caution on the citation itself: Bhardwaj, Gupta, and Howell is a working paper, not yet a peer-reviewed journal article, so its findings should be cited as what they are — the best available causal evidence, from a credible design, but still subject to revision. That is exactly the standard this series holds itself to: a paper is cited when it can be verified, and its status is stated plainly.

## 6. The Famous Example: HCA, 2010

No recap story is told more often than HCA, because it contains the whole argument in one company. In November 2006, Bain Capital, KKR, and Merrill Lynch's private equity arm — with the founding Frist family co-investing — took HCA, the largest for-profit hospital operator in the United States, private in a roughly $33 billion deal, the largest LBO of its era. The sponsors put in about $5.3 billion of equity against roughly $28 billion of debt, at the peak of the buyout boom, months before the financial crisis.

HCA survived the crisis — hospitals, it turns out, are a resilient business. By early 2010 the credit market had reopened, and the sponsors faced a choice: exit through an IPO into a still-shaky equity market, or use the strengthening credit market to lever the company back up and pay themselves. They did both, but the recap came first, in three instalments: $1.75 billion in January 2010, $500 million in May, and $2 billion in November — **$4.25 billion in a single year, about 80% of the sponsors' original equity, returned before a single share had been sold.** The November dividend was funded largely by a $1.5 billion junk bond issue, and the payout lifted HCA's leverage ratio from 4.52x to an estimated 4.88x.

HCA then went public in March 2011 at $30 a share, raising $3.8 billion, and the sponsors made a large profit on the IPO and the sell-down. The recap did not sink the company — HCA is still a large public company today. But notice what the numbers say about the risk taken: the sponsors extracted dividends equal to roughly 140% of the company's free cash flow over the preceding four years, and the company was re-levered while still carrying the debt of the largest LBO ever done. The deal worked because the business was resilient and the credit market stayed open. The recap did not create that resilience; it bet on it, with the sponsors' money already back in their pockets.

HCA is the best-case recap, and it is still a useful warning: it is remembered as a success because the downside never arrived. A banker who uses HCA to justify a recap in a cyclical business, at higher leverage, in a shakier credit market, is using the right example for the wrong deal.

## 7. Smart vs. Reckless: A Checklist

A recap is a judgement call, and judgement calls deserve a checklist. Before recommending one, a banker should be able to answer yes to all five:

**1. Coverage with room to spare.** After the recap, interest coverage should still be comfortable through a downturn, not just in the base case. Run the stress test from section 4 — EBITDA down 25% — and check both leverage and coverage. If the company breaches a covenant in the stress case, the recap is too big.

**2. The business is not cyclical at the wrong moment.** Recaps work best in stable, cash-generative businesses — healthcare services, software with recurring revenue, essential infrastructure. They are most dangerous in cyclical businesses at the top of the cycle, when EBITDA is flattered and leverage looks lower than it really is. Borrowing against peak earnings is the classic recap mistake.

**3. Growth is funded first.** The recap dividend should come from *excess* debt capacity — capacity left over after growth capex, maintenance, and working capital are funded. If the dividend means cancelling a plant expansion or deferring maintenance, the sponsor is trading the company's future for the fund's present.

**4. Covenant headroom is real.** Post-recap leverage should sit comfortably inside the incurrence test, not pressed against it. Headroom of half a turn of EBITDA is not headroom; it is a rounding error away from a breach.

**5. Everyone is told the truth about the numbers.** The LPs should know the IRR went up because cash came back early, not because the deal got better. The new lenders should know the dividend is the use of proceeds. And the sponsor should be able to say, out loud, what happens if EBITDA falls 25% — because if that answer is uncomfortable, it is the answer that matters.

## 8. Common Misconceptions

**"A recap creates value."** It moves value, and it moves risk. The total value of the firm is roughly unchanged — Modigliani–Miller again — except for the tax shield on the extra interest and the time value of the earlier distribution. The sponsor's gain is the company's increased risk, and the interest drag in section 4 is the receipt for it.

**"The IRR went up, so the deal got better."** The IRR went up because cash arrived earlier. MOIC — the total dollars returned per dollar invested — fell once the interest was counted. A recap improves the *speed* of the return, not necessarily its *size*. Any presentation that shows only the IRR is hiding the MOIC.

**"Recaps only happen in bad deals."** The opposite: they happen in the *good* deals, the ones whose earnings grew and whose debt was paid down enough to create room to borrow again. That is the selection effect the research had to correct for — and it is why "the company could handle it" is never sufficient evidence that a recap was wise.

**"The lenders agreed, so it must be safe."** The new lenders agreed because they were paid to take the risk. The *old* lenders did not agree — they watched their claims get diluted, and the research finds their loan prices fall when a recap happens.

**"A dividend is a dividend."** A dividend paid from earnings distributes value the company created. A dividend paid from new debt distributes value the company *hopes* to create. The accounting looks identical. The economics are not.

**3.** A sponsor proposes a recap that takes leverage from 4.0x to 6.5x EBITDA, just inside a 6.5x incurrence test, in a cyclical business at what looks like peak earnings. Name three things wrong with this proposal, using the checklist in section 7.

**4.** Bhardwaj, Gupta, and Howell (2024) find that dividend recaps increase deal returns but reduce fund returns, reduce wages among surviving firms, and reduce pre-existing loan prices.
