# 26 | Equity Capital Markets Beyond the IPO: Follow-Ons, Blocks, and Buybacks

*Article 19 showed you how a company goes public — the roadshow, the bookbuild, the first-day pop. But here is the thing nobody tells you at the IPO party: going public is not a one-time event. It is the beginning of a long relationship between a company and the equity market. Listed companies come back to that market again and again — to raise more money, to let early investors sell, and to buy their own shares back. This article is about those three moves, and about the surprisingly consistent ways the market reacts to each of them.*

> **Learning goals.** After this article you can: (1) distinguish a follow-on offering, a block trade, and a buyback, and say who sells what in each case; (2) compute the dilution from a follow-on — ownership, EPS, and book value per share — and find the break-even return on the new money; (3) explain why stocks usually fall when a company announces an equity issue, and why they usually rise when it announces a buyback; (4) describe how a block trade is priced and executed overnight; (5) compare open-market repurchases, tender offers, and accelerated share repurchases, and explain when each one makes sense.
>
> **Prerequisites.** Article 03 (enterprise value vs equity value) for share counts and market capitalisation, article 09 (accretion/dilution) for the EPS math that reappears here, article 19 (IPO pricing and bookbuilding) for how bankers build a book and price a deal, and article 20 (debt capital markets) for the other side of the financing choice. Myers and Majluf (1984), cited in the README's paper list, is the theoretical backbone of section 3.

## 1. The IPO Is Not the End of the Story

When a company lists, the bankers on the deal — the **equity capital markets** (ECM) team — do not disappear. ECM is the product group that handles everything a listed company does with its own shares after the IPO: raising more equity, helping big shareholders sell, and buying shares back. If DCM (article 20) is the debt side of corporate finance, ECM is the equity side, and the two groups spend a surprising amount of time competing for the same client. A CFO who needs $500 million can borrow it, issue shares, or do a bit of both, and the ECM banker and the DCM banker will each argue that their product is the right one.

The reason this matters is that a listed company's shares are a living currency. Unlike a private company, which raises equity in discrete, negotiated rounds, a public company can tap the market almost any week of the year. That flexibility is valuable — and it is also dangerous, because every use of that currency sends a signal. Issue shares and the market wonders whether you think your stock is overvalued. Buy shares back and the market wonders whether you have run out of growth ideas. The whole game of ECM is managing those signals while getting the mechanics right. There are three basic moves. A **follow-on offering** (also called a seasoned equity offering, or SEO) is when the company itself issues new shares and takes in the cash. A **block trade** is when an existing shareholder — a private equity fund, a founder, a strategic investor — sells a large chunk of shares to the market, with the company receiving nothing. A **buyback** (share repurchase) is when the company itself buys its own shares and cancels them, shrinking the share count. New shares in, old shares out, shares cancelled: that is the full menu, and almost every ECM transaction is a variation on one of the three.

## 2. Follow-On Offerings: How a Listed Company Raises More Equity

Start with the simplest question: why would a company that is already public need more equity? The honest answer is usually one of three reasons. It is funding growth — an acquisition, a new plant, a product launch — and equity is the right funding for a risky investment whose returns are uncertain. It is repairing its balance sheet — paying down debt after a bad year, the way article 13's distressed companies wish they had done earlier. Or it is opportunistically taking advantage of a high share price, which is a polite way of saying management thinks the stock is expensive and would rather sell now than later. The market knows all three motives exist, and it spends a lot of effort figuring out which one applies — which is the subject of section 3.

The mechanics resemble the IPO from article 19, but faster. A **fully marketed follow-on** runs a short roadshow — a few days of meetings with investors — and then prices, much like a mini-IPO. An **accelerated bookbuild** (ABB) skips the roadshow entirely: the deal is announced after the market closes, the book is built overnight, and the shares are priced before the market opens the next morning. The ABB exists because speed has value: a company that needs to fund an acquisition announced yesterday cannot wait two weeks for a roadshow, and every day of delay is a day for the share price to move against it. The trade-off is that an ABB gives investors less time to do homework, so it usually prices at a slightly wider discount than a marketed deal.

There is a third variant, the **bought deal**, in which the bank buys the entire offering itself at a fixed price and then resells it. The company gets certainty — the money is committed before the deal is announced — and the bank takes the risk that it cannot resell at a profit. Bought deals are common in Canada and the UK, and rarer in the US, where the ABB dominates. In every variant, someone absorbs price risk in exchange for a fee, and the size of that fee tells you how much risk was involved.

One more piece of machinery carries over from the IPO: the **greenshoe**, or overallotment option. The underwriters can sell up to 15% more shares than the base offering and then buy them back in the market, or exercise the option to buy them from the company, depending on where the price goes. In a follow-on it works exactly as in article 19 — it lets the bank stabilise the price in the first days of trading and gives the company a little extra capital if demand is strong.

Now the arithmetic, which is where most people's intuition goes wrong. When a company issues new shares, two things happen at once: the share count rises, and the cash balance rises. Whether existing shareholders are better or worse off depends on what the company does with the cash, not on the share count alone. That is the whole point of section 6, so keep it in mind: dilution of ownership is automatic; dilution of value is not.

## 3. Why the Stock Usually Falls When a Company Issues Shares

Here is one of the most reliable patterns in corporate finance: when a listed company announces it will issue new shares, its stock price falls — on average, by about 3% for industrial companies on the announcement, according to Asquith and Mullins (1986), who studied seasoned equity offerings and found that the announcement-day price drop was larger the bigger the offering relative to the company's size. The fall is not a law of nature, but it is consistent enough that bankers plan around it, and the explanation is the most important idea in this article.

The explanation starts with an asymmetry. Management knows more about the company's true prospects than outside investors do. That is not a cynical claim; it is simply true — the CEO sees the order book, the pipeline, and the problems that have not yet shown up in the accounts. Now suppose management believes the shares are undervalued. Would it sell new shares at a bargain price? No — it would wait, or borrow instead, and let the market discover the good news first. Suppose instead management believes the shares are overvalued. Then issuing shares is a gift: the company sells something worth $35 for $40. So when the market sees an equity issue announced, it rationally updates its beliefs: the company is more likely to be the kind that thinks its stock is expensive. The price falls, not because the offering itself destroys value, but because the announcement reveals information.

This is the **adverse selection** logic of Myers and Majluf (1984) — *Corporate Financing and Investment Decisions When Firms Have Information That Investors Do Not Have*, published in the *Journal of Financial Economics* — and it is the reason for the **pecking order** of corporate finance: companies prefer to fund themselves first from retained earnings, then from debt, and only last from new equity, because equity is the funding source that carries the worst signal. The pecking order is not a rule companies follow consciously; it is the equilibrium that emerges when every announcement is read for what it implies.

Three qualifications keep this from being a crude rule. First, the signal depends on the stated use of proceeds. An issue to fund a named acquisition, where investors can judge the deal themselves, typically falls less than an issue for "general corporate purposes" — a phrase the market reads as "we need the money and we would rather not say why." Second, young, fast-growing companies fall less, because everyone already expects them to need equity. Third, the fall is about the announcement, not the issue itself: by the time the shares are actually sold, the price has already adjusted. A useful discipline: whenever you see a follow-on announced, ask what the market learned, not just what the company raised.

## 4. Block Trades: Selling a Lot, Fast

Not every ECM transaction involves the company. Often it is a shareholder who wants out — and wants out fast. A private equity fund that took a company public three years ago still owns 15% of it. It wants to sell half that stake to return money to its own investors. It cannot simply dump 8 million shares on the open market: the selling pressure would crush the price, and it might take weeks. Instead it does a **block trade**.

The mechanics are deliberately unglamorous. After the market closes, the bank calls a small group of large investors — this is called **wall-crossing**, because the investors are brought "over the wall" and told confidential information (that a big seller is coming) under an agreement not to trade on it until the deal is public. The bank collects orders overnight, agrees a price, and the trade is announced before the market opens. The shares change hands in one block, and by the time ordinary investors wake up, it is done.

The price is almost always at a **discount** to the previous close — typically 2% to 5% for a liquid stock, more for a large block or a less liquid one. The discount is the payment for two things: the buyer takes on a large position in one go, and the seller gets certainty and speed. Think of it as the mirror image of the follow-on discount in section 2, but with one crucial difference: a block trade does not change the share count. No new shares are created, no cash enters the company, and earnings per share do not change. What changes is only who owns the shares. That is why the market usually treats a block trade more calmly than a follow-on — the seller's motives are about its own portfolio, not about the company's prospects — although a very large sale by an insider still makes people ask questions.

One more distinction worth filing away: a block trade can also be structured as a **secondary offering**, a registered, marketed sale of existing shares that looks like a follow-on but with the proceeds going to the selling shareholder. The difference is mostly one of speed and formality — a block is overnight and unmarketed, a secondary is a fuller process.

## 5. Buybacks: The Mirror Image

If issuing shares is the move a company makes when it thinks its stock is expensive, buying shares back is the move it makes when it thinks its stock is cheap — or at least when it has cash and nothing better to do with it. A **share repurchase** (buyback) is the company spending its own cash to buy its shares in the market and cancel them. The share count falls, each remaining share owns a bigger slice of the company, and earnings per share rise even if earnings themselves do not change. That last point is the one that gets quoted most, and also the one most often misunderstood, as section 6 will show.

There are three ways to do it. The most common is the **open-market programme**: the board authorises, say, $500 million of repurchases over the next year, and the company buys shares gradually in the open market, often through a **10b5-1 plan** — a pre-arranged trading schedule that lets it buy even during periods when insiders are not allowed to trade, because the decisions were made in advance. The programme is flexible — the company can pause it at any time — and the market knows that, so the announcement effect is modest.

The second is the **tender offer**: the company offers to buy a fixed number of shares at a fixed price, usually a premium of 10% to 20% over the market price, for a limited window. Tender offers are fast and decisive — the shares disappear in weeks — but they cost more, because the premium is the price of speed and certainty.

The third is the **accelerated share repurchase** (ASR): the company pays a bank upfront, the bank delivers most of the shares immediately (borrowing them in the market), and then buys shares over the following months to cover its position, settling the difference with the company at the end. An ASR is the buyback equivalent of a bought deal — immediate impact, bank takes the execution risk — and companies use it when they want the EPS benefit recognised now rather than spread over a year.

Why buy back shares instead of paying dividends? Flexibility and taxes: a dividend creates an expectation of permanence (cut it and the market punishes you), while a buyback can be stopped silently; and in many tax systems, a buyback's capital gain is taxed more lightly than dividend income. The signalling answer is the mirror of section 3: announcing a buyback says "we think our shares are undervalued," and the market usually agrees, at least a little. But the evidence goes further than the announcement. Ikenberry, Lakonishok, and Vermaelen (1995) studied open-market repurchase announcements from 1980 to 1990 and found that the average abnormal buy-and-hold return over the four years after the announcement was 12.1% — and for "value" stocks, the ones most likely to be genuinely undervalued, it was 45.3%. The market underreacts to the signal: it does not fully believe the company at first, and the outperformance accrues slowly, over years, as the undervaluation becomes visible.

But be careful about what a buyback does *not* do. It does not create value by itself. If a company buys back shares at a price above their true value, it destroys value for the shareholders who remain — it has spent $40 of their cash on something worth $35. The EPS increase is arithmetic, not magic: earnings are divided by fewer shares, but the cash used to buy those shares is gone, and with it the interest it earned or the investment it could have funded. A buyback is a good use of cash when the shares are cheap and the company has no better investment; it is a bad use when the shares are expensive or when the money should have gone to the business. The question is never "did EPS go up?" — it almost always does. The question is "was the price paid below the value received?"

## 6. Worked Example: Harborline Raises Money, Then Buys It Back

Now let us run all three moves through one company, with every number shown, so you can see exactly what changes and what does not.

Meet **Harborline Technologies** (fictional). It is listed, profitable, and boring in the best way:

| | Before any deal |
|---|---|
| Shares outstanding | 100 million |
| Share price | $40.00 |
| Market capitalisation | $4,000 million |
| Net income | $300 million |
| Earnings per share (EPS) | $3.00 |
| Book value of equity | $2,000 million |
| Book value per share (BVPS) | $20.00 |

### The follow-on

Harborline announces it will issue 12.5 million new shares — 12.5% of the existing count — to fund an acquisition. The stock falls about 3% on the announcement, to $38.80, consistent with the Asquith and Mullins (1986) average. The deal is priced at $38.00, a 5% discount to the pre-announcement price:

```math
\text{Gross proceeds} = 12.5\text{m} \times \$38.00 = \$475\text{m}
```

Underwriting fees are 3% of gross proceeds, or $14.25 million, so net proceeds are $460.75 million. The share count rises to 112.5 million, and existing shareholders' ownership falls:

```math
\text{Existing holders' ownership} = \frac{100\text{m}}{112.5\text{m}} = 88.9\%
```

That 11.1 percentage-point fall is the dilution of *ownership*, and it is unavoidable. But what happens to EPS depends entirely on what Harborline earns on the $460.75 million. The break-even return — the return on the new money that leaves EPS exactly unchanged — is:

```math
r^* = \frac{\text{EPS}_{\text{old}} \times \Delta S}{\text{Net proceeds}} = \frac{\$3.00 \times 12.5\text{m}}{\$460.75\text{m}} = 8.14\%
```

The logic: the new shares need to earn their share of the EPS. Twelve-and-a-half million new shares at $3.00 of EPS each require $37.5 million of extra net income, and $37.5 million on $460.75 million of new money is 8.14%. If the acquisition earns more than that, EPS rises; if less, EPS falls:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\text{At a 10\% return: } \text{NI} &= \$300\text{m} + 10\% \times \$460.75\text{m} = \$346.1\text{m}, \quad \text{EPS} = \frac{\$346.1\text{m}}{112.5\text{m}} = \$3.08 \\
\text{At a 5\% return: } \text{NI} &= \$300\text{m} + 5\% \times \$460.75\text{m} = \$323.0\text{m}, \quad \text{EPS} = \frac{\$323.0\text{m}}{112.5\text{m}} = \$2.87
\end{aligned}
```

So the same offering is accretive at a 10% return and dilutive at 5% — the share count tells you nothing on its own. Book value per share, meanwhile, rises, because the issue price ($38.00) is above the old book value per share ($20.00):

```math
\text{New BVPS} = \frac{\$2,000\text{m} + \$460.75\text{m}}{112.5\text{m}} = \$21.87
```

Issue above book value and BVPS rises; issue below it and BVPS falls. That is the same principle as in the accretion/dilution analysis of article 09, applied to the balance sheet instead of the income statement. And the greenshoe, if exercised in full at 15% of the base deal, would add 1.875 million more shares and $71.25 million of gross proceeds.

### The block trade

Six months later, an early private equity investor sells 8 million shares — 8% of the company — in an overnight block trade at $38.40, a 4% discount to the $40.00 close:

```math
\text{Block value} = 8\text{m} \times \$38.40 = \$307.2\text{m}
```

Nothing else changes. The share count stays at 112.5 million, EPS stays at whatever the acquisition delivered, and Harborline receives no cash. The only thing that moved is ownership: the seller is out, and a group of institutional buyers is in. Compare that with the follow-on above: the block is cheaper for the market to absorb precisely because it creates no dilution and reveals little about the company.

### The buyback

A year later, Harborline's acquisition has worked out, the share price is back at $39.00, and the board authorises a $300 million open-market repurchase programme. The company buys shares at an average price of $39.00:

```math
\text{Shares repurchased} = \frac{\$300\text{m}}{\$39.00} = 7.69\text{m}
```

The share count falls from 100 million (suppose, for clarity, we run this on the pre-follow-on numbers) to 92.31 million, and EPS rises even though net income is unchanged:

```math
\text{New EPS} = \frac{\$300\text{m}}{92.31\text{m}} = \$3.25 \quad \text{(up 8.3\% from \$3.00)}
```

But book value per share falls, because the buyback price ($39.00) is above the old book value per share ($20.00):

```math
\text{New BVPS} = \frac{\$2,000\text{m} - \$300\text{m}}{92.31\text{m}} = \$18.42 \quad \text{(down from \$20.00)}
```

This is the pair of results to hold in your head: a buyback above book value is EPS-accretive and BVPS-dilutive at the same time. Both statements are true, and neither by itself tells you whether the buyback created value. What decides that is whether $39.00 was below the shares' true value. If Harborline's shares were genuinely worth $45, the buyback was a bargain and the remaining shareholders are richer; if they were worth $35, the company just spent its owners' money overpaying for its own stock. The EPS headline cannot distinguish the two cases. Only a valuation can.

For completeness, a tender offer would work the same way at a higher price: an offer for 5 million shares at $44.00 — a premium of roughly 13% to the $39.00 market price — would cost $220 million, cut the share count to 95 million, lift EPS to $3.16, and reduce BVPS to $18.74. The premium is the cost of doing it all at once.

| Transaction | New shares? | Cash to company? | EPS effect | BVPS effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Follow-on (12.5m at $38) | Yes, +12.5m | +$460.75m net | Depends on return on proceeds (break-even 8.14%) | Rises to $21.87 (issue price > BVPS) |
| Block trade (8m at $38.40) | No | None | None | None |
| Buyback ($300m at $39) | No, −7.69m | −$300m | Rises to $3.25 | Falls to $18.42 (price > BVPS) |
| Tender offer (5m at $44) | No, −5m | −$220m | Rises to $3.16 | Falls to $18.74 |

## 7. What the ECM Banker Actually Does

Strip away the jargon and the ECM banker's job has four parts. First, **advice**: when a CFO asks "should we raise equity or debt?", the banker runs the math of section 6, weighs the signalling cost of section 3 against the flexibility of a buyback, and gives an answer that survives the board meeting. Second, **execution**: structuring the deal (marketed vs accelerated vs bought), drafting the prospectus, and managing the timetable so the deal prices into a calm market rather than a storm. Third, **distribution**: the bookbuild itself — calling investors, collecting orders, deciding who gets shares and at what price, the skill article 19 described for IPOs and which works the same way here, only faster. Fourth, **aftercare**: stabilisation through the greenshoe, research coverage, and the ongoing relationship that determines whether the company calls this bank or another one next time.

## Common Misconceptions

**"A follow-on always destroys value because it dilutes shareholders."** Ownership dilution is real and automatic; value dilution is not. If the new money earns more than the break-even return — 8.14% in Harborline's case — EPS rises and the deal creates value despite the extra shares. Judge the use of proceeds, not the share count.

**"A falling share price on the announcement means the offering is a bad idea."** The fall is the market updating its beliefs about what management knows, per Myers and Majluf (1984). A good acquisition funded by an issue that the market initially punishes can still be the right decision. The announcement reaction is information; it is not a verdict on the investment.

**"Buybacks always create value because EPS goes up."** EPS goes up by arithmetic whenever shares are cancelled — that is not evidence of anything. A buyback creates value only if the shares are bought below their true value. Buying your own stock at an inflated price destroys value for the shareholders who stay, however flattering the EPS headline looks.

**"A block trade means the seller knows something bad."** Sometimes, but usually not. A private equity fund sells because its fund is maturing, a founder sells because of estate planning, an index fund sells because of a rebalancing. The block structure exists precisely because these are portfolio decisions, not company news — which is why the market's reaction is normally muted.

**"Issuing equity is always more expensive than issuing debt because the discount is bigger."** The costs are of different kinds: debt has a contractual interest bill and a maturity, equity has dilution and a signalling cost, and the right comparison runs through the break-even return and the pecking order, not through the size of one day's discount. Article 20's bond pricing and this article's follow-on math are two halves of the same financing decision.

## Test Yourself

**1.** Harborline (100m shares, $300m net income, $40.00 price) issues 12.5m shares at $38.00, with 3% fees. What are the net proceeds, the new share count, and the break-even return on the new money for EPS to stay at $3.00?
*Answer: Gross proceeds $475m; fees $14.25m; net proceeds $460.75m. New share count 112.5m. Break-even return = ($3.00 × 12.5m) / $460.75m = 8.14%.*

**2.** Why does the stock usually fall when a follow-on is announced, even if the money will fund a good project? Name the paper and the mechanism.
*Answer: Adverse selection — Myers and Majluf (1984). Management knows more than investors; it would rather issue when shares are overvalued than undervalued, so the announcement itself signals possible overvaluation and the market marks the price down. The fall reflects the signal, not necessarily the quality of the project.*

**3.** Harborline buys back $300m of shares at $39.00 when book value per share is $20.00. How many shares are cancelled, what happens to EPS, and what happens to BVPS? Does the EPS increase prove the buyback created value?
*Answer: 7.69m shares cancelled; shares fall to 92.31m; EPS rises from $3.00 to $3.25; BVPS falls from $20.00 to $18.42. No — the EPS rise is arithmetic. Value creation depends on whether $39.00 was below the shares' true value.*

**4.** A PE fund sells 8m Harborline shares in an overnight block at a 4% discount to a $40.00 close. What is the block worth, and why does this sale not change EPS — while a follow-on of the same size would?
*Answer: 8m × $38.40 = $307.2m. A block trade transfers existing shares, so the share count, net income, and EPS are all unchanged. A follow-on creates new shares, changing the share count and (depending on the return on proceeds) EPS.*
