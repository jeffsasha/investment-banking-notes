# 39 | Take-Privates: When Public Companies Go Private

*Every article so far has treated the target as a private company — a founder-owned business, a division being sold, a small competitor with no stock ticker. But the biggest leveraged buyouts in history were not like that at all. They were public companies, with thousands of shareholders, quarterly earnings calls, and a share price quoted every second. Dell, Hilton, Twitter: each of them was taken private, bought out from under the public market entirely. This article is about how that works — why a sponsor would pay a premium to own a company the market already owns, what changes when the target is listed, and why the arithmetic is both simpler and more dangerous than a private-company buyout.*

> **Learning goals.** After this article you can: (1) define a take-private and explain how it differs from buying a private company; (2) list the six reasons companies go private, and say which ones the evidence actually supports; (3) walk through the process — approach, special committee, go-shop, tender offer vs. merger, and the shareholder vote; (4) build a take-private sources-and-uses and compute MOIC and IRR, including what the premium costs the sponsor; (5) explain what the research finds about the premiums public shareholders receive and where they come from; (6) name the four ways a take-private goes wrong.
>
> **Prerequisites.** Article 03 (enterprise value vs equity value) for the bridge; article 05 (precedent transactions) for control premiums; article 08 (LBO) for sources and uses, MOIC and IRR; article 10 (football field) for how an offer price is triangulated; article 13 (restructuring) for what happens when the leverage goes wrong.

## 1. The Idea in One Paragraph

A **take-private** — also called a **public-to-private (PTP)** transaction — is a leveraged buyout in which the target is a publicly listed company. A private equity sponsor (sometimes with the company's own management, in a **management buyout** or **MBO**) offers to buy all of the outstanding shares, usually in cash, at a price above the current market price. If shareholders approve, the company is delisted, the shares stop trading, and the business continues as a private company owned by the sponsor, funded largely with debt. That is the whole transaction. What makes it different from every deal in this series so far is the starting point: the target already has a price. The market has valued it, in public, every trading day for years — and the sponsor has to pay more than that price, in full view of everyone, to make it go away.

That public price changes everything. In a private-company deal, the price is whatever two parties negotiate in a room, and nobody else sees it. In a take-private, the price is a premium to a quoted number, it will be printed in the press the morning the deal is announced, and every shareholder gets to vote on whether it is enough. The sponsor cannot quietly buy a bargain and hope nobody notices. It has to explain, to a board, to shareholders, and often to a judge, why the price is fair. And it has to be right, because the leverage that makes the deal work also makes the mistake permanent.

## 2. Why Go Private? Six Reasons, Ranked by Evidence

Companies do not go private on a whim. Being public has real benefits — liquidity for shareholders, a currency for acquisitions, access to capital — so the decision to leave usually means one of the following has become more valuable than all of that:

**1. Undervaluation.** The most cited reason, and the one with the strongest evidence behind it. Management believes the market is pricing the company below what it is actually worth — because the story is complicated, because the sector is out of favour, because a restructuring will take three years and the market only looks one quarter ahead. Going private lets the buyer capture the gap between the market price and the true value. Notice the tension: the sponsor pays a premium *above* the market price, but still believes it is buying *below* intrinsic value. The premium and the bargain are the same number, seen from two sides.

**2. The cost of being public.** A listed company pays for audits, SEC filings, investor relations, a board with independent directors, Sarbanes-Oxley compliance, and the management time all of it consumes. For a small company those costs can run to several million dollars a year — real money against a modest earnings base. This reason is real but rarely decisive on its own; nobody takes a company private just to save the audit fee.

**3. Short-termism.** Public markets punish a bad quarter. A CEO who needs to close a plant, rebuild a product line, or endure two years of lower earnings to fix the business will be punished for it while the stock is listed. In private hands, the same plan can be executed without a quarterly earnings call explaining it to people who will sell the stock before the plan works. This is the reason managers themselves give most often — and it is also the hardest to verify, because every CEO believes the market is too short-term about *their* company.

**4. Incentive realignment.** In a public company, management owns a small fraction of the equity and is paid largely in salary and options that vest on a schedule. In a private company owned by a sponsor, management typically rolls a large part of its wealth into the deal and owns a meaningful slice of the new equity. The sponsor's theory is that this concentrates the mind: when your own money is on the line, you run the business differently. This is the same agency logic behind article 08's LBO, and it is the mechanism the research finds most consistently.

**5. The tax shield.** The debt that funds the buyout generates interest payments, and interest is tax-deductible. A company that was paying corporate tax on its earnings can shelter a large part of them with the interest on its new debt. This is a genuine source of value — but it is a one-off gain from financial engineering, not from running the business better, and it depends on the debt actually being serviced.

**6. Control and flexibility.** A private owner can make decisions quickly — sell a division, change the strategy, hire or fire — without a shareholder vote, an activist campaign, or a hostile bid. For a company that needs to be restructured rather than managed, that speed matters. It is also, honestly, part of the appeal for management: no more public scrutiny.

Which of these actually drive the deals? That is a research question, and section 5 answers it. For now, notice the pattern: the first three are about the *market* getting something wrong or costing something; the last three are about the *owner* getting something better. A good take-private thesis usually has at least one of each.

## 3. How a Take-Private Actually Works

The process is an M&A process (articles 11 and 12) with extra machinery bolted on, because the target has thousands of owners instead of one. The steps, in order:

**The approach.** The sponsor approaches the CEO or the board, usually privately, with an indicative offer. If management wants the deal, it often participates — an MBO — which immediately creates a conflict of interest: the same people who are supposed to negotiate the highest price for shareholders are also the buyers. This conflict is why the rest of the machinery exists.

**The special committee.** Because management is conflicted, the board forms a **special committee** of independent directors — directors with no stake in the buyout — to evaluate the offer. The committee hires its own bankers and its own lawyers, separate from the company's advisers. This is the single most important governance feature of a take-private: it is the shareholders' only protection against management buying the company cheaply from itself.

**The fairness opinion.** The committee's bankers produce a **fairness opinion** (article 10) — an analysis that the offer price is fair, from a financial point of view, to the public shareholders. It is built on the same valuation methods this series has covered: comps, precedent transactions, DCF. It is not a guarantee that the price is the *best* price, only that it falls within a defensible range. Still, it is the document a court will look at first if shareholders sue.

**The go-shop.** After the deal is signed, the target typically gets a **go-shop period** — usually 30 to 45 days — during which it is allowed to actively solicit competing offers. This exists precisely because the special committee knows the first bidder may not be the highest. If a better offer appears, the target can switch, paying a break fee to the original buyer. Go-shops rarely produce a topping bid — bidders are reluctant to enter a process where someone else has already done the diligence — but they matter, because their existence is what makes the price defensible in court.

**Tender offer vs. merger.** The buyer acquires the shares in one of two ways. In a **tender offer**, the buyer offers to buy shares directly from shareholders, and if it reaches a threshold (usually 90% under Delaware law), it can squeeze out the rest in a short-form merger. In a **one-step merger**, shareholders vote on the deal at a meeting, and a majority (usually) approves it. The tender offer is faster; the merger is simpler. Either way, the endpoint is the same: the company is delisted, and every shareholder receives the same cash price per share.

**The shareholder vote and appraisal rights.** Public shareholders get a vote, and the deal needs their approval. Shareholders who think the price is too low have a remedy: **appraisal rights**, the right to ask a Delaware court to determine the fair value of their shares. Appraisal litigation is a real risk for the buyer — courts have sometimes awarded more than the deal price — and it is one of the reasons the fairness opinion and the go-shop matter so much.

**The financing.** Like any LBO, the purchase is funded with debt and equity — but at public-company scale, which is where take-privates get dangerous. A $10 billion take-private needs several billion dollars of debt committed by banks *before* the deal is announced, and that commitment has to survive the months between announcement and close. If credit markets freeze in between, the buyer can be trapped: contractually committed to buy, unable to borrow. The buyer's escape hatch is the **reverse termination fee** — a fee (often 3–6% of the deal value) it pays to walk away. It is the price of the option to abandon the deal, and in a bad market it is the cheapest way out.

Two legal concepts a banker on the deal must know by name. **Revlon duties** (from the 1986 Delaware case *Revlon v. MacAndrews & Forbes*): once a board decides to sell the company, its duty shifts from defending the company to getting the highest price reasonably available for shareholders. A board that accepts the first offer without testing the market has breached that duty. And **Rule 13e-3**: the SEC rule governing going-private transactions, which requires detailed disclosure of the purpose of the transaction, the alternatives considered, and whether the deal is fair to unaffiliated shareholders. The paperwork is the price of taking a company away from its public owners.

## 4. The Worked Example: Meridian Packaging Group

Let us build the whole thing, because the premium — the number that makes a take-private different — is also the number that makes or breaks the return. **Meridian Packaging Group** is a fictional listed packaging company. All numbers are in millions of dollars, except per-share prices.

**The starting point.** Meridian has 40 million shares outstanding, trading at $25.00.

```math
Market capitalisation = 40m × $25.00 = $1,000m
Net debt = $400m
Enterprise value = $1,000m + $400m = $1,400m
EBITDA = $175m
Trading EV/EBITDA = $1,400m / $175m = 8.0x
```

**The offer.** The sponsor offers $32.50 per share in cash — a 30% premium to the market price, a typical take-private premium. The board's special committee negotiates it up from an initial $30.00, and the fairness opinion concludes it is within a defensible range.

```math
Equity purchase price = 40m × $32.50 = $1,300m
Premium paid = $1,300m − $1,000m = $300m
Offer EV = $1,300m + $400m = $1,700m
Offer EV/EBITDA = $1,700m / $175m = 9.7x
```

Stop and look at that last line. The sponsor is paying 9.7x EBITDA for a company the market values at 8.0x. The $300 million premium is not a fee or a rounding error; it is the cost of buying the company away from its shareholders, and it comes straight out of the sponsor's return. In a private-company LBO (article 08), there is no market price to beat, so no premium is paid. In a take-private, the premium is the first line of the return calculation — and it is negative.

**Sources and uses.** The purchase price is $1,300 million of equity, plus the existing $400 million of net debt must be refinanced at close, plus $50 million of fees and expenses.

| Uses | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Purchase of equity (40m shares × $32.50) | $1,300m |
| Refinancing of existing net debt | $400m |
| Fees and expenses | $50m |
| **Total uses** | **$1,750m** |

| Sources | Amount | Multiple of EBITDA |
|---|---:|---:|
| Term loan | $700m | 4.0x |
| High-yield bonds | $350m | 2.0x |
| Sponsor equity | $700m | — |
| **Total sources** | **$1,750m** | **6.0x total debt** |

Total debt of $1,050 million is 6.0x EBITDA — aggressive, but standard for a take-private of a stable, cash-generative business. The sponsor's equity cheque is $700 million.

**The exit.** Five years later, the sponsor sells Meridian. The plan worked: EBITDA has grown to $230 million — roughly 5.6% a year, from modest organic growth and two small bolt-on acquisitions. The business sells at 9.0x EBITDA, below the 9.7x entry multiple (because the sponsor paid a premium at entry, it does not get to keep the full multiple) but above the 8.0x the market had applied before the deal.

```math
Exit EV = $230m × 9.0 = $2,070m
```

Over five years, free cash flow — after interest, taxes, and maintenance capex — pays down $60 million of debt a year, so debt at exit is $1,050m − $300m = $750m.

```math
Exit equity value = $2,070m − $750m = $1,320m
MOIC = $1,320m / $700m = 1.89x
IRR = 1.89^(1/5) − 1 ≈ 13.5%
```

Check the IRR: 1.135^5 = 1.135² × 1.135² × 1.135 = 1.288 × 1.288 × 1.135 ≈ 1.88, close to 1.89. A 1.89x multiple of invested capital and roughly a 13.5% IRR — a solid but not spectacular private equity return.

Now the counterfactuals, because the base case assumed the plan worked. The table shows what happens when it does not:

| Scenario | Exit EV | Exit equity | MOIC | IRR |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Base case: EBITDA $230m, exit at 9.0x | $2,070m | $1,320m | 1.89x | ~13.5% |
| No exit re-rating: EBITDA $230m, exit at 8.0x | $1,840m | $1,090m | 1.56x | ~9.3% |
| Flat business: EBITDA $175m, exit at 8.0x | $1,400m | $650m | 0.93x | ~−1.5% |

Check the middle row: $230m × 8.0 = $1,840m; minus $750m of debt is $1,090m; $1,090m / $700m = 1.56x; and 1.56^(1/5) − 1 ≈ 9.3%. Check the bottom row: $175m × 8.0 = $1,400m — exactly the pre-deal enterprise value — minus $750m of debt is $650m; $650m / $700m = 0.93x, a loss of about 1.5% a year.

The bottom row is the one to stare at. If the business does not grow at all and the exit multiple is just the multiple the market had applied all along, the sponsor loses money — even though the business itself is worth exactly what it was worth before the deal. Why? Because the sponsor paid a $300 million premium and $50 million of fees to buy it, and the debt paydown of $300 million only just offsets the premium. The premium is a hole the sponsor has to climb out of before it makes a dollar. In a private-company LBO, there is no hole. In a take-private, the hole is the first thing on the page.

That is also why the leverage matters so much. The deal only works if the EBITDA growth actually arrives *and* the exit multiple holds. If either fails, the return collapses — and if both fail, the sponsor is underwater on a business that did nothing wrong.

## 5. What the Research Says

Two papers frame the debate, one American and one British, and they agree on the shape of the answer while disagreeing on the emphasis.

**Bharath and Dittmar (2010)**, *"Why Do Firms Use Private Equity to Opt Out of Public Markets?"* Review of Financial Studies, studied a comprehensive sample of US going-private transactions from 1980 to 2004. Their central finding is about information and liquidity: the firms that go private are the ones for which the public market works worst — firms whose shares are thinly traded, whose information is hard for outsiders to evaluate, and whose value the market struggles to price. On average, the firms in their sample went public about thirteen years before going private, and the factors that predicted the decision were visible at the IPO: low liquidity and high information costs from the start. Access to capital and control considerations grew in importance over the company's public life. The implication is uncomfortable for the "public markets are efficient" view: going private is, in part, a response to the market failing to do its job for a particular kind of company.

**Renneboog, Simons, and Wright (2007)**, *"Why Do Public Firms Go Private in the UK? The Impact of Private Equity Investors, Incentive Realignment and Undervaluation,"* Journal of Corporate Finance, studied UK public-to-private transactions from 1997 to 2003. Their headline numbers are worth remembering: pre-transaction shareholders received, on average, a premium of about 40% over the pre-announcement price, and the share price reaction to the announcement was about 30%. Then they tested where that premium comes from, and three sources dominated: **undervaluation** of the target before the deal, the **interest tax shield** from the new debt, and **incentive realignment** — the value created by putting management's wealth at risk. Notably, an expected reduction in free cash flow (the Jensen argument from article 08, that debt disciplines managers who would otherwise waste cash) did *not* determine the premiums, and take-privates were *not* a defensive reaction against hostile takeovers.

Read together, the two papers tell a coherent story. The companies that go private are the ones the public market undervalues and under-serves, and the premium their shareholders receive is paid for by a combination of that undervaluation being corrected, the tax benefit of the new debt, and management being given a sharper incentive to perform. What the papers do *not* find is the simple morality tale in either direction: not that sponsors are stealing companies from their shareholders, and not that going private magically creates value. The premium is real — 30–40% is a lot of money — but the research says it is mostly a transfer of value that was already there, plus a tax benefit, plus better incentives. Whether the sponsor earns a good return on top of the premium depends on what happens next, which is section 4's table, not a paper.

## 6. How Take-Privates Go Wrong

Four failure modes, each with a famous example:

**1. The premium was too high.** The sponsor paid 40% over a market price that was, in fact, about right. The undervaluation thesis was wrong, and the $300 million hole in section 4 never gets filled. This is the most common failure, and the hardest to admit, because the premium was the deal's most public number — the sponsor announced it, defended it, and paid it. The market price is a piece of information, not just an obstacle: when the market says 8.0x and you pay 9.7x, you need a reason, and "we will manage it better" is the weakest reason in the book.

**2. The debt was too much.** Take-privates are the largest LBOs, and the largest LBOs carry the most debt. When the cycle turns, the debt that made the deal possible becomes the thing that kills it. Hilton's 2007 take-private by Blackstone — $26 billion, at the peak of the buyout boom — nearly collapsed in the financial crisis, and survived only because Blackstone renegotiated the debt and waited out the cycle. The deal eventually made money. Many of its contemporaries did not.

**3. The deal breaks before close.** Between announcement and close, anything can happen: the financing market freezes, a material adverse change appears, the buyer gets cold feet. The most famous example is Twitter in 2022: Elon Musk agreed to buy it for $44 billion, tried to walk away, and was forced to close by the Delaware court. The reverse termination fee exists for exactly this situation, and whether it is cheaper to pay it or to close is a calculation the buyer's lawyers make in real time. A broken take-private leaves the target's share price in ruins and its management exhausted — the worst outcome for everyone except the lawyers.

**4. The public scrutiny never really ends.** Going private is supposed to free management from the quarterly grind. But a take-private of a famous company attracts regulators, journalists, politicians, and litigants in a way a private-company deal never does. The fairness opinion gets challenged, the appraisal litigation drags on for years, and the sponsor discovers that the public market's scrutiny has been replaced by the courtroom's. Dell's 2013 take-private — $24.9 billion, led by Michael Dell and Silver Lake — was fought over for months by shareholders who argued the price was too low, including Carl Icahn, and the appraisal fight continued long after the deal closed.

None of these is a reason not to do a take-private. They are reasons to price one honestly: the premium is a cost, the debt is a risk, and the process is a negotiation with thousands of counterparties who can say no.

## 7. Common Misconceptions

**"A take-private means the market was wrong about the company."** Sometimes. But the market price is a piece of evidence, not a mistake to be assumed. Renneboog and co-authors found undervaluation was a real source of the premium — but only one of three, alongside the tax shield and incentive realignment. A sponsor that assumes the market is wrong, without evidence, is paying a premium for its own opinion.

**"The premium is a gift to shareholders."** It is a price, not a gift. Shareholders receive it because they are giving up the company's future upside — including the upside the sponsor expects to create. If the sponsor's plan works, the shareholders sold too cheap; if it fails, they sold at the right time. You cannot know which until the exit, which is years later.

**"Going private means no more scrutiny."** It means *different* scrutiny: a special committee, a fairness opinion, a shareholder vote, appraisal litigation, SEC filings under Rule 13e-3, and — for the famous deals — the press and politicians. The quarterly earnings call disappears. The courtroom does not.

**"Management buyouts are the same as any other buyout."** They are not, because management is on both sides of the table. That is why the special committee exists, why the go-shop exists, and why courts look at MBOs more skeptically than arm's-length deals. A banker advising the special committee is doing a different job from a banker advising the sponsor — and confusing the two is how fairness opinions get challenged.

**"The leverage is the same as in a private-company LBO."** The mechanics are the same, but the scale and the risk are not. A take-private borrows against a company whose value is quoted daily, whose shareholders can sue, and whose debt must be committed before the deal is announced. When the credit market moves between signing and close, the buyer can be caught with a commitment it cannot fund — which is why the reverse termination fee matters, and why the biggest take-privates are also the biggest financing risks in the market.

**3.** Renneboog, Simons, and Wright (2007) found that UK public-to-private shareholders received an average premium of about 40%. Name the three sources their tests found actually explain that premium — and one explanation their tests did *not* support.

**4.** A sponsor is considering a take-private of a company trading at 7.0x EBITDA. It expects to exit at 8.0x EBITDA in five years, with EBITDA growing 4% a year, and debt paydown of $40 million a year. Before doing any arithmetic, what is the single most important question to ask about the deal — and why does the answer matter more than the exit multiple?
