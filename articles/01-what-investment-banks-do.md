# 01 | What Investment Banks Actually Do: Business Lines, Deal Types, and the Banker's Toolkit

*This is where it all starts. Before any valuation model or deal multiple, there's a simpler question worth answering honestly: what does an investment bank actually sell? The answer is less glamorous than the reputation — and more interesting.*

> **Learning goals.** After this article you can: (1) name the four main divisions of an investment bank and what each sells; (2) distinguish advisory from underwriting; (3) list the five canonical deal types and how the bank earns a fee on each; (4) describe what an analyst actually does all day.
>
> **Prerequisites.** Basic accounting vocabulary (revenue, profit, assets, liabilities). No finance background required — this series starts from zero.

## 1. The One-Sentence Definition

An investment bank is a **financial intermediary**: it sits between parties that need capital or want to transact, and it gets paid a fee for making the transaction happen or advising on it.

That is the whole business. Everything else — the prestige, the long hours, the jargon — is commentary on this sentence. Two verbs matter: **advise** and **underwrite**.

- **Advise** = tell a client what to do (sell the company? buy the rival? at what price?) and execute the process. The bank risks its reputation and time, not its balance sheet.
- **Underwrite** = put the bank's own capital at risk to guarantee a result. In an IPO, the bank buys the shares from the issuer and resells them to investors; if investors won't pay enough, the bank eats the difference.

Most revenue at most banks comes from the first verb. Remember this — it explains the entire incentive structure of the industry.

## 2. The Four Divisions (and Where "Investment Banking" Sits)

People say "investment banking" to mean two different things: the whole firm, and one division inside it (the Investment Banking Division, IBD). The four divisions:

| Division | What it sells | Who the client is | Revenue model |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Investment Banking Division (IBD)** | Advice + execution on M&A, IPOs, debt raises, restructurings | Corporations, PE firms, governments | Success fees, retainers |
| **Sales & Trading (Markets)** | Liquidity: executes trades, makes markets, structures derivatives | Institutional investors, hedge funds | Bid-ask spread, trading P&L |
| **Research** | Analysis and recommendations on stocks/bonds | Investors (internal + external) | Soft dollars, supports trading & IBD credibility |
| **Asset / Wealth Management** | Manages money for a fee | Pension funds, wealthy individuals | % of assets under management |

**Coverage vs. product groups (inside IBD).** IBD itself splits two ways. *Coverage* bankers own relationships by industry (Technology, Healthcare, Energy, FIG…) or geography. *Product* bankers own a technical skill used across industries: M&A, ECM (equity capital markets), DCM / Leveraged Finance (debt), Restructuring. A Healthcare M&A banker and a Technology M&A banker do the same *product* work for different *coverage* clients. As an analyst you typically sit in one product or coverage group.

## 3. The Five Canonical Deal Types

Nearly every IBD mandate is one of these:

1. **Sell-side M&A** — advising a company (or PE fund) selling itself or a division. The bank runs an auction, builds the valuation, negotiates. Fee: typically ~0.5–1.5% of deal value, paid only if the deal closes ("success fee").
2. **Buy-side M&A** — advising an acquirer. Fewer bidders to manage, more strategy work: is the target worth it, what synergies are real, what price still creates value?
3. **IPO / Equity offerings (ECM)** — taking a company public or raising equity. The bank underwrites: it prices the shares, builds the order book, and guarantees the proceeds.
4. **Debt financing (DCM / Leveraged Finance)** — issuing bonds or arranging loans, including the debt that funds leveraged buyouts. Fees are thinner than M&A but volumes are huge.
5. **Restructuring** — advising a distressed company or its creditors when the debt can't be paid as agreed. Counter-cyclical: this group gets busy precisely when M&A slows down.

Notice the pattern: deals 1–4 happen when markets are optimistic; deal 5 when they aren't. That cyclicality is why banks keep all five.

## 4. How the Bank Actually Gets Paid

A simplified fee logic, because incentives explain behavior:

- **M&A advisory:** success fee scaled to deal value (the famous "Lehman formula" heritage: ~1% on large deals, higher percentages on small ones), sometimes plus a monthly retainer credited against the success fee. No close, no fee — which is why bankers push hard to get deals *done*.
- **Underwriting (IPO/bonds):** the "gross spread" — historically ~7% on US IPOs (now often lower for large deals), ~0.5–1% on investment-grade bonds. The bank earns it by buying low from the issuer and selling higher to investors.
- **Restructuring:** monthly fees plus a success fee on the completed restructuring.

Two consequences worth internalizing now: (a) bankers are paid on *closed* transactions, so their advice structurally favors doing the deal; (b) because fees scale with size, bankers prefer larger deals — a $5bn sale at 0.8% pays the same team far more than a $500m sale at 1.2%.

## 5. What an Analyst Actually Does All Day

Strip away the mystique and the analyst job is three outputs, repeated:

1. **Pitch books** — slide decks that win mandates ("hire us") or advance a live deal. 80% of junior life.
2. **Models** — Excel valuation and merger models: comps, DCF, LBO, accretion/dilution. The quantitative spine of every pitch and every deal.
3. **Process work** — buyer lists, data rooms, management presentations, CIMs (confidential information memoranda).

The models are the point of this series. A pitch book *argues*; a model *proves* (or at least disciplines the argument). Articles 04–10 will build each valuation method from first principles, and articles 11–13 will put them inside real deal processes.

## 6. Why Valuation Is the Through-Line

Every deal type above reduces to one question: **what is this company worth, and to whom?**

- Sell-side: what will buyers pay? (Precedent transactions, LBO analysis — what a PE buyer *can* pay.)
- Buy-side: what can we afford to pay and still create value? (Accretion/dilution, DCF with synergies.)
- IPO: at what price will public investors buy? (Trading comps.)
- Restructuring: what is the enterprise worth versus what is owed? (Distressed valuation decides who gets what.)

Four methods answer that question — **comparable companies, precedent transactions, DCF, and LBO** — and bankers triangulate them on a "football field" chart. You will be able to build all four by article 10.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Investment bankers pick stocks / invest the bank's money."** No — that's asset management or proprietary trading. IBD bankers advise and intermediate; they don't run a portfolio. (Volcker Rule-era regulation further separated these.)
- **"M&A bankers and traders do the same job."** Opposite sides of the wall: advisory is relationship- and process-driven over months; trading is market- and risk-driven over seconds. They even sit behind a "Chinese wall" of information barriers.
- **"Banks only do deals for huge corporations."** Middle-market and boutique banks do sub-$500m deals all day; the mechanics are identical, the fees are percentage-higher.
- **"Valuation gives one true price."** Valuation gives a *range* and a *negotiating position*. Anyone promising a single true number is selling something.

## Test Yourself

**Q1.** A bank advises a PE fund buying a chemicals company and also arranges the debt financing the fund uses. Which two IBD products are involved, and on what is each fee based?
*A1. Buy-side M&A (success fee on closed deal value) and Leveraged Finance/DCM (fee on debt arranged). The bank earns twice on one transaction — a common cross-sell.*

**Q2.** Why might a restructuring group have its best year during a recession while the M&A group has its worst?
*A2. Recessions create distressed companies that need restructuring advice (counter-cyclical demand), while healthy M&A requires confident buyers, available financing, and agreeable valuations — all scarce in downturns.*

**Q3.** In an IPO, what does "underwrite" mean concretely, and who bears the risk if investors won't pay the offer price?
*A3. The bank commits to buy the shares from the issuer at the offer price (minus spread) and resell them. If demand is weak, the bank is left holding shares worth less than it paid — the bank bears the risk.*

**Q4.** A banker tells a board "your company is worth $800m–$950m." Is this evasive, or is it the correct professional answer? Why?
*A4. Correct. Value depends on buyer, method, and assumptions; a defensible range triangulated across methods (the "football field") is more honest and more useful in negotiation than a false-precision point estimate.*
